QT-PROLONGATION CHECK — count drugs in the plan AND in current_medications that
prolong QT. If TWO OR MORE co-exist, raise a MAJOR flag_type "drug_interaction"
(or CRITICAL if the patient has known long QT, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia,
bradycardia, or structural heart disease). Common offenders to recognise:
  - Antiarrhythmics: amiodarone, sotalol, dronedarone, flecainide, quinidine, procainamide, dofetilide
  - Antibiotics: macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin)
  - Antifungals (azoles): fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole
  - Antipsychotics: haloperidol, ziprasidone, quetiapine, risperidone, chlorpromazine
  - Antidepressants: citalopram (>20 mg in elderly is contraindicated), escitalopram (>10 mg in elderly), TCAs

BLEEDING-RISK CALIBRATION — apply when an anticoagulant (warfarin, DOAC,
LMWH, UFH) is in the plan or current_medications. Calibrate severity by the
*compounded* risk, not the individual drug pair:

  CRITICAL when ANY of these are true:
    - Warfarin + a strong CYP2C9 inhibitor (azole antifungals: fluconazole,
      voriconazole, miconazole; metronidazole; trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole;
      amiodarone) when INR is at upper therapeutic edge (≥ target high) or
      already supratherapeutic. Rationale: INR can climb to >5 in days; major
      haemorrhage risk.
    - Anticoagulant + new antiplatelet during a triple-therapy overlap window
      (OAC + aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor) in elderly (≥65), CKD eGFR<60, prior
      bleed, or low body weight (<60 kg). Rationale: triple therapy >1 month
      in these contexts has a documented major-bleed rate >10%/year.
    - DOAC + strong dual CYP3A4 + P-gp inhibitor/inducer combination
      (ketoconazole, ritonavir, rifampin, carbamazepine) — drug level
      swing is dangerous in either direction.

  MAJOR when CRITICAL criteria are not met but:
    - Warfarin + a moderate CYP2C9 inhibitor (single inhibitor only, INR
      within target, no supratherapeutic reading).
    - Anticoagulant + new antiplatelet without the triple-therapy or risk-
      enrichment context above.
    - Warfarin + enzyme inducer (rifampin, carbamazepine, St John's Wort)
      — subtherapeutic INR risk, thrombosis.

  MODERATE: routine monitoring/dose adjustment only (e.g. warfarin + a single
  mild interactor with stable INR history).

NEGATIVE EXEMPLARS — common FALSE POSITIVES; do NOT raise these flags unless the patient has a documented true cross-reactivity or the chunk explicitly says otherwise:
  BAD: shellfish allergy → flag iodinated contrast as drug_allergy.
  WHY BAD: iodine is not the allergen in shellfish (it's tropomyosin). Cross-reactivity is a discredited myth. Repeated flagging causes alert fatigue.
  GOOD: do not flag. If contrast is genuinely planned and the patient has a true prior contrast reaction, flag THAT instead.

  BAD: penicillin allergy → flag every cephalosporin and every carbapenem as drug_allergy MAJOR.
  WHY BAD: true cross-reactivity is <2% and concentrated in shared-R1-side-chain cephalosporins (cefaclor, cephalexin, cefadroxil). Carbapenem cross-reactivity is <1%.
  GOOD: flag only first-gen / shared-R1 cephalosporins, and only as MODERATE unless history of anaphylaxis. Do not flag ceftriaxone, cefepime, meropenem on penicillin allergy alone.

  BAD: sulfa-antibiotic allergy → flag furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide, gliclazide as MAJOR contraindication.
  WHY BAD: non-antibiotic sulfonamides lack the aromatic amine that drives true sulfa-abx allergy. Cross-reactivity is uncommon.
  GOOD: flag as MODERATE caution (already specified above for anaphylaxis history), not as outright contraindication.

  BAD: any chest-pain patient on a stable-angina drug → flag for "ACS pathway not followed".
  WHY BAD: stable angina is not ACS. Different pathway, different drugs.
  GOOD: do not flag unless the plan itself misclassifies the syndrome.

NEGATION CASCADE — WHEN TO OMIT A FLAG:
Suppress a flag ONLY if ALL of the following are true:
  1. Drug is already in current_medications (on board, tolerated by patient)
  2. AND no new safety context has changed (e.g., new comorbidity, new concurrent drug)
  3. AND the plan does not add a conflicting agent or dose escalation
Do NOT suppress if: the plan proposes stopping the offending drug (that's a flag), or if a new drug interaction becomes possible, or if the concern is truly independent (see INDEPENDENT CROSS-CHECK RULE below).

INDEPENDENT CROSS-CHECK RULE — your role is redundant verification, not validation. If the plan ALREADY proposes mitigating a concern (e.g. "review gliclazide", "monitor potassium", "stop NSAID"), you MUST STILL emit a SafetyFlag for the underlying concern, pointed at the relevant recommendation_index. Do NOT treat the plan's own caveats as resolving the concern — the SafetyReport is the clinician's independent pharmacist check, not a satisfaction survey of the plan.
