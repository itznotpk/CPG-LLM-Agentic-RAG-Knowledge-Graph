# CHAPTER 6: PROJECT SELF-EVALUATION

## 6.1 Reflections on Design

The central design decision — deterministic wherever possible, generative only where genuine clinical reasoning is required — proved to be the right governing principle, but its costs were underestimated at the outset.

The deterministic layers delivered exactly what they were intended to deliver. Stage 3's D1–D6 routing ladder resolves ICD-11 codes to CPG documents via explicit scope tables, and the scope-refusal mechanism produces a first-class `out_of_scope` event rather than a confident answer built on retrieved text that has no clinical authority for the presented case. Both behaviours are auditable at the row level: each routing decision logs the D-level that matched or rejected, each scope refusal logs the maximum semantic similarity and the threshold that was not crossed. These properties are not achievable in a pure-retrieval system, and they are the reason the system can be trusted to stay silent rather than confabulate on out-of-scope cases. The design succeeded here precisely because the decision to be deterministic was made architecturally, not as a prompt instruction.

The generative layers were harder to make reliable. Stage 5 synthesis — the LLM call that produces the care plan — is where the faithfulness gap (0.864 vs ≥0.90 target) lives. The root cause is not prompt failure; it is that the synthesis model paraphrases clinical background knowledge that was not present in the retrieved chunks, because instruction-tuned models cannot fully suppress their pre-training distribution. The design response — an independent Stage 6 faithfulness critic and the acute-scope fix that blocks KG-injected comorbidity referrals on acute presentations — reduced the gap but did not close it. In retrospect, an earlier investment in faithfulness measurement (the full n=30 independent-judge run was completed only late in the project) would have enabled more targeted interventions.

The dual-grounding architecture — pgvector for CPG chunks and Neo4j for typed drug-interaction edges — is the design decision with the clearest clinical payoff. Case 9 and Case 10 both surface safety flags that a single-grounding system cannot produce: KG-sourced interactions that the retrieved CPG paragraphs do not explicitly discuss, and teratogen vetoes on drugs already in the patient's medication list. The implementation of these two stores as independent sources merged at Stage 6 — rather than as a single concatenated context — is what makes the dual-source claim architecturally true rather than cosmetic.

The seven-stage pipeline produced one design tension that was not resolved cleanly: latency. Each stage adds wall time, and two of the seven stages involve heavy LLM calls (Stage 2 DDx re-ranking and Stage 5 synthesis). The end-to-end mean of ~2.5 minutes is the direct consequence of a sequential synchronous pipeline designed for correctness rather than speed. The design is correct in the sense that matters clinically — the plan it returns is grounded, cited, and safety-checked — but at ~2.5 minutes it cannot fit the 10-minute rural consultation in its current form. This is a design debt, not an implementation error, and it requires an architectural response (streaming synthesis, stage parallelism where dependencies allow) rather than a parameter tweak.

The Pydantic-validated TreatmentPlan schema was a correct early design decision whose payoff compounded throughout the project. Because every Stage 5 output is a typed object with a fixed section structure, Stage 6 can perform structured safety checks (not regex searches), the frontend can render consistently, and the faithfulness evaluator can decompose the plan into discrete claim units for scoring. Designing the plan as a schema rather than a prose blob made every downstream layer simpler and more testable.

One design gap that became visible only during evaluation is the absence of a coverage metric for the drug knowledge graph. The KG surfaces interactions for the medication space present in the 30-CPG corpus, but no audit has been performed to quantify what fraction of clinically significant interactions in that space it covers. Without a recall number, the safety-critic's KG arm cannot be characterised beyond "it flags what the graph knows." Designing a KG coverage audit into the evaluation plan from the beginning would have converted this from a limitation into a measured boundary.

## 6.2 Reflections on Implementation

The implementation phase surfaced four patterns worth recording.

**The evaluation harness revealed real bugs.** The six silent-degradation and infrastructure probes (SIL/INF) were added late in the implementation, when the pipeline was believed to be stable. The first run exposed four fail-silent bugs: a reranker JSON-parse failure that produced no degraded signal, a zero-chunk retrieval that returned a plan with confidence 0.92 and no caveats, a Stage 4 exception that passed through to synthesis on empty evidence, and a pgvector connection refused that returned a generic HTTP 500. None of these would have been caught by unit tests on the happy path. The lesson is that safety-contract testing — probing what the system does when a dependency fails, not just when it succeeds — should be designed into the testing plan from the beginning, not added as a late check.

**Gold-set quality determines what the metrics measure.** The Layer A2 routing evaluation began with a headline Top-1 accuracy of 18.2%, which appeared to indicate a severe routing defect. Investigation showed the actual defects were in the gold set: wrong ICD codes for several conditions, non-existent sub-codes, and a title matcher that failed on format differences between gold strings and system output. After correcting the gold and normalising the matcher, the routing accuracy was 100%. The Layer C Stage-4 negative lift (−0.173 Recall@20) was similarly traced to a gold set designed for Layer B single-query evaluation being fed to a multi-query pipeline — the metric was measuring gold-construction bias, not retrieval quality. Investing in gold-set correctness early, and building harnesses that flag gold-set anomalies (zero-pool conditions, non-existent IDs), would have reduced the diagnostic cost of these artefacts.

**Determinism is a layered property, not a binary one.** The determinism work (§4.13, cases 8–11) produced a more nuanced finding than a pass/fail verdict: the primary diagnosis is stable where a single dominant diagnosis exists (top-1 = 10/10 for cases 8 and 9), the Stage-2 candidate query is byte-identical across runs, and the residual variance is precisely localised to the seedless Gemini reranker and the non-deterministic synthesis model. This required running n=10 per case rather than a single re-run, and tracking stability at three levels (top-1, Jaccard top-5, safety flags) rather than one. The implementation effort for the determinism harness was modest; the insight it produced — that the system has a well-defined determinism surface and a well-defined stochastic surface — was worth the investment.

**The degree of success against specifications.** Against the four objectives in §1.3, the implementation met three fully (knowledge base, safety enforcement, care-plan schema) and one partially (auditable second opinion within the consultation window, where the reasoning traceability is complete but the in-consult speed is not). Against the published quantitative targets in VALIDATION.md, the implementation passed 7 of 13 rows outright, missed 3 rows by small margins (faithfulness −0.036, nDCG@10 −0.081, Precision@5 structural), and identified 2 rows whose published targets were calibrated for a different system architecture than was built (p95 latency `< 8 s` for a two-LLM-call pipeline; DDx exact Hit@5 ≥ 0.90 where lineage Hit@5 = 0.971 but exact = 0.771). The application-tier test suites and multi-clinician validation remain incomplete.

## 6.3 Environmental Considerations and Sustainability

A clinical decision support system of this architecture has a non-trivial environmental footprint, and honest self-evaluation requires acknowledging it.

The computational cost is dominated by the two LLM inference calls in Stage 2 (DDx reranking, ~22 s mean) and Stage 5 (synthesis, ~61 s mean). These calls are routed to cloud-hosted inference endpoints (Gemini and MiMo); each inference run draws on data-centre energy at an efficiency determined by the provider's hardware and energy mix. For a system designed for rural Malaysian clinics — where a consultation may be the sole clinical contact a patient has in months — the energy expenditure per recommendation is difficult to argue against on proportionality grounds. The clinical benefit of a correctly grounded care plan that prevents a medication error or avoids an unnecessary referral likely outweighs the marginal energy cost of the LLM call that produced it.

That said, there are specific design choices that reduce the environmental footprint without reducing clinical value:

**Scope refusal eliminates inference on out-of-scope cases.** When Stage 3 returns `out_of_scope`, the pipeline halts before Stage 5's LLM call. In clinics that encounter presentations outside the 30-CPG corpus regularly, this design choice reduces unnecessary inference without any clinical cost — the system is correctly silent rather than expensively wrong.

**Deterministic stages dominate the early pipeline.** Stages 1, 3, and parts of 4 are deterministic SQL and vector operations with negligible energy cost compared to LLM calls. Keeping the LLM calls to the minimum necessary for the task — rather than routing every step through a language model — is both a correctness and an efficiency choice.

**Caching repeat patterns.** The prior-visit summary loop reduces the information that must be re-derived on each consultation. Future implementation of DDx and routing caches for common chief-complaint patterns would further reduce per-consultation inference cost in high-volume clinics.

**Infrastructure efficiency.** The backend runs on a Neon serverless Postgres instance (pgvector) and an Aura-hosted Neo4j graph — both cloud-managed with active resource scaling, so the database layer does not hold idle compute. The FastAPI backend is containerisable for deployment on energy-efficient cloud infrastructure.

The longer-term sustainability of the system depends on maintenance of the CPG corpus. Malaysian MOH CPGs are periodically revised; each revision requires re-ingestion, re-chunking, and a regression run of the evaluation harness to confirm that routing and retrieval quality are not degraded. Designing the ingestion pipeline to be low-friction and the eval harness to be runnable without specialist knowledge are sustainability investments that reduce the per-update cost of keeping the system current.

## 6.4 Addressing the Local Community: Rural Malaysian Primary Care

The project was motivated by, and designed for, a specific community: rural and district primary care clinicians in Sabah and Sarawak, and the patients they serve under conditions of systematic resource constraint.

Every architectural choice in the system was informed by that context. The CPG corpus is exclusively Malaysian MOH guidelines — not American Heart Association or European Society of Cardiology guidelines adopted without local adaptation. The DDx and routing evaluation gold sets use ICD-11 codes verified against the Malaysian clinical context. The safety-critic logic reflects Malaysian prescribing practice, not global defaults. The Doctor UI was designed for a solo medical officer or medical assistant working under time pressure, not for a specialist in a resource-rich tertiary centre.

The three faces of clinical decision isolation identified in §1.2 — no colleague, no guideline, no pharmacist — map directly onto three architectural capabilities. The DDx stage and the care-plan synthesis address the absence of a colleague by providing a structured second opinion grounded in evidence. The CPG routing and retrieval stages address the absence of a usable guideline by surfacing the relevant sections from the locally validated corpus within the consultation. The dual-source safety critic addresses the absence of a pharmacist by performing a structured medication audit — checking the patient's existing and proposed medications against both LLM pharmacological reasoning and a typed graph of interactions and contraindications.

These are not generic clinical AI features. They are a response to a documented pattern of care in Malaysian rural practice: the 39.3% CPG non-adherence rate driven by time constraints and search friction [6]; the 88% second-assessment revision rate among complex cases [3]; the medication-related harm that accounts for roughly half of preventable harm globally when no pharmacist is present [18]. ClearPath was designed to be the second pair of eyes that rural clinics structurally lack, not to replace the clinician.

The cultural and contextual dimensions of the local community also shaped what the system does not do. The scope is primary care within the 30-CPG corpus — the system does not attempt to cover traditional or complementary medicine, does not generate recommendations outside its validated scope, and produces a first-class refusal event (rather than a low-confidence answer) when a case falls outside its competence. This is a deliberate cultural choice: in a setting where a wrong recommendation from a trusted system could cause significant harm with no specialist to catch it, silence is safer than reach. The `out_of_scope` event is not a failure mode; it is the system acknowledging the boundary of its validated knowledge and routing the decision back to the clinician's judgement.

The single expert evaluation conducted (Universiti Malaya, n=1) provided a meaningful data point. The evaluator confirmed that ClearPath's clinical content and safety surfacing match a strong LLM baseline, that reasoning transparency and override control are excellent, and that the primary gap for rural adoption is speed and output simplification. This feedback directly shapes the priority ordering in §5.5: latency reduction and summary-mode UI are the first-order engineering tasks before any further capability expansion.

The validation gap — the absence of a blinded multi-clinician evaluation with real rural practitioners — is acknowledged explicitly. Clinical evidence that ClearPath improves decisions under real rural-consultation conditions has not been collected; the system has demonstrated that its recommendations are guideline-grounded, safety-checked, and clinician-endorsed in a structured evaluation setting, but the step from laboratory validation to demonstrated clinical impact requires deployment, IRB, and a prospective study that is beyond the scope of this project. That step is the next one.

The project's contribution to the local healthcare context is therefore best described as a credible infrastructure artefact: a functional, evaluated, and honestly documented clinical decision support system designed specifically for Malaysian rural primary care, ready for the deployment and prospective validation work that will determine whether it improves care in practice. That is what the evidence supports, and that is what this report claims.
