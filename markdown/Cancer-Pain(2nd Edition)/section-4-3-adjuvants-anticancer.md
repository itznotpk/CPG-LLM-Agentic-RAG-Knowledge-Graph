## 4.5 Adjuvants

Adjuvant analgesics are medications with primary indications other than pain. However, they are useful in managing certain painful conditions, particularly neuropathic pain. Evidence for the use of these medications was largely extrapolated from studies on non-cancer pain.

| Class | Primary Use in Cancer Pain |
|---|---|
| **Anticonvulsants** | Neuropathic pain |
| **Antidepressants** | Neuropathic pain |
| **Corticosteroids** | Inflammation-related pain |
| **Bone targeting agents** | Painful bone metastases |
| **Ketamine** | Refractory pain (adjunct to opioids) |

---

### Anticonvulsants

#### Evidence for Efficacy (Meta-analysis, Neuropathic Pain Including Cancer)

| Drug | Dose Range | NNT (50% pain relief) | NNH |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gabapentin** | 900-3600 mg/day | 6.3 (95% CI 5.0 to 8.3) | 25.6 (95% CI 15.3 to 78.6) |
| **Pregabalin** | 150-600 mg/day | 7.7 (95% CI 6.5 to 9.4) | 13.9 (95% CI 11.6 to 17.4) |

**Key findings:**
*   No dose-response effect for gabapentin; pregabalin showed better response at higher doses
*   Combination therapy of gabapentin with morphine was superior to monotherapy
*   Studies using other antiepileptic agents were mostly negative
*   GRADE recommendation: **strong**

#### Cancer-Specific Evidence

| Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Cochrane SR (small RCT) | Gabapentin 1800 mg and pregabalin 600 mg decreased pain scores, had morphine-sparing effect and improved functional capacity | Very low |
| Meta-analysis (tumour-related pain) | Adding gabapentin or pregabalin to stable opioid analgesia did not improve pain intensity | Low |

#### Adverse Effects

*   63% on gabapentin (≥1200 mg/day) experienced at least one AE vs 49% on placebo (RR=1.3, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.4; NNH=7.5, 95% CI 6.1 to 9.6)
*   Most common AEs: somnolence, dizziness, peripheral oedema and ataxia/gait disturbances

---

### Antidepressants

#### Evidence for Efficacy (Meta-analysis, Neuropathic Pain Including Cancer)

| Drug | NNT (50% pain relief) | NNH | Evidence Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amitriptyline** | 3.6 (95% CI 3.0 to 4.4) | 13.4 (95% CI 9.3 to 24.4) | Moderate |
| **SNRIs** (duloxetine, venlafaxine) | 6.4 (95% CI 5.2 to 8.4) | 11.8 (95% CI 9.5 to 15.2) | High |

*   No dose-response effect for amitriptyline

#### Cancer-Specific Evidence

| Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Cochrane SR (2 trials, cancer neuropathic pain) | Only one trial showed amitriptyline 50-100 mg decreased mean pain intensity, had morphine-sparing effect and improved functional capacity | Very low |
| SR (chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy) | Duloxetine more effective than placebo (MD=0.73, 95% CI 0.26 to 1.20) | Low (GRADE) |
| SR (tumour-related cancer pain) | Adding amitriptyline to stable opioid analgesia did not improve pain relief | Low |

#### Adverse Effects

*   55% on amitriptyline experienced at least one AE vs 36% on placebo (RR=1.5, 95% CI 1.3 to 1.8; NNH=5.2, 95% CI 3.6 to 9.1)
*   Most common AEs: somnolence, dizziness, dryness of mouth, nausea and constipation

> **Previous CPG & WHO Guidance:** Neuropathic cancer pain may be treated with antidepressants and/or anticonvulsants. Despite the lack of high-quality evidence, WHO also suggests practitioners may consider these for patients with inadequate pain relief or intolerable AEs to opioids.

---

### Corticosteroids

Due to their anti-inflammatory mechanism, corticosteroids are used as adjuvant analgesics for pain associated with inflammation.

**Indications:**
*   Headache from brain metastases
*   Abdominal pain from liver capsule distension or intestinal obstruction
*   Neuropathic pain from spinal cord compression

| Evidence Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Cochrane SR | Corticosteroids more effective than controls in cancer pain for up to one week (MD= -0.84, 95% CI -1.38 to -0.30) |
| Same review | Improvement in quality of life or patient well-being also reported |

**Common AEs:** Restlessness, insomnia, GI and CV events, Cushingoid facies, anxiety, fluid retention, hypocalcaemia and hyperglycaemia.

> **Clinical Note:** Current evidence is insufficient to establish an ideal dose, duration or route. Clinicians should prescribe cautiously: carefully assess the benefit, treat for the shortest duration and discontinue early if ineffective.

---

### Bone Targeting Agents

Bisphosphonates inhibit osteoclast activity and are used to prevent or delay skeletal-related events (SRE) in patients with bone metastases. They have been found to reduce pain and analgesic requirements in certain cases. Examples include clodronate, ibandronate, pamidronate, risedronate, etidronate and zoledronate.

Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody directed against RANK ligand, leading to decreased osteoclast activity and reduced bone resorption.

#### Evidence by Cancer Type

| Cancer Type | Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NSCLC** | SR (bisphosphonates & denosumab) | No high-level evidence that any agent reduced/prevented pain or improved QoL | — |
| **Breast cancer** | Cochrane SR | Bisphosphonates appeared to reduce bone pain vs placebo in 6 of 11 studies | Moderate (GRADE) |
| **Prostate cancer** | Cochrane network meta-analysis | See detailed findings below | — |

#### Prostate Cancer — Detailed Findings (Cochrane Network Meta-analysis)

| Outcome | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Pain response** | Zoledronate ranked best, followed by etidronate, clodronate and risedronate. Only zoledronate was more effective than clodronate (RR=1.19, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.39). No trial on denosumab in pair-wise comparison. |
| **Renal impairment** | Zoledronate increased risk vs placebo (RR=1.63, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.45); clodronate showed no significant risk. No significant differences between agents. |
| **Osteonecrosis of jaw (ONJ)** | Denosumab increased ONJ vs placebo (RR=3.45, 95% CI 1.06 to 11.24); zoledronate and clodronate showed no significant difference. |
| **Grade 3-4 AEs** (fatigue, diarrhoea, nausea) | Only denosumab increased AE risk vs placebo (RR=1.46, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.99). Zoledronate had lower AE risk vs denosumab (RR=0.92, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.98). |

#### WHO Guidelines on Bone Targeting Agents

*   **Recommends:** Bisphosphonates for prevention and treatment of bone pain in adults (including older persons) and adolescents with bone metastases
*   **No recommendation** for or against monoclonal antibodies for bone pain
*   **No recommendation** for or against comparative advantage of monoclonal antibodies over bisphosphonates

### Others (Ketamine)

Ketamine is an NMDA-receptor antagonist used for general anaesthesia and sedation. It can also be used in selected patients whose pain has been inadequately relieved by opioids alone.

| Evidence Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Cochrane SR | Insufficient evidence for clinical benefit of ketamine as adjuvant to opioids for cancer pain |
| Same review (RCT) | Twice the incidence of AEs with rapid dose escalation |
| AEs | Hallucinations and cognitive disturbance at higher doses |

> **Previous CPG Guidance:** Ketamine may be used by specialists familiar with cancer pain management or palliative medicine/pain specialists.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 13**
> * Anticonvulsants or antidepressants may be considered in patients with neuropathic cancer pain.
> * Corticosteroids may be used cautiously as an adjuvant in patients with specific cancer pain syndromes.
> * Bone targeting agents may be used in cancer patients with painful bone metastasis.

## 4.6 Medical Cannabis

Medical cannabis is a term used to describe cannabis used for medical purposes. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the potential therapeutic use of cannabis for various medical conditions including chronic pain. However, there is still much debate surrounding its safety and effectiveness.

Evidence mapping of systematic reviews reported that the evidence was broad, highly heterogeneous in methodology and with conflicting conclusions. There was a limited number of studies investigating cancer pain relief by medicinal cannabis.

### Evidence Summary

| Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-analysis (5 RCTs, cannabis + opioids) | Non-significant trivial reduction in cancer pain (WMD= -0.18 cm on 10 cm VAS, 95% CI -0.38 to 0.02) | High certainty |
| Same meta-analysis (AEs) | Increased nausea (RR=1.43, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.96) and vomiting (RR=1.50, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.24) | Moderate certainty |
| Meta-analysis (4 RCTs, cannabis vs placebo) | Non-significant pain reduction (WMD= -0.1, 95% CI -0.28 to 0.09); significantly higher risk of transient cognitive impairment, vomiting, drowsiness, impaired attention and nausea | — |
| Malaysian HTA | Current evidence inadequate to recommend medical cannabis in cancer pain | — |
| 2023 Meta-analysis (RCTs) | No significant difference vs placebo in pain reduction or serious AEs | Low (GRADE) |

> **Conclusion:** There is insufficient evidence to formulate a recommendation for medical cannabis use in cancer pain.

## 4.7 Anticancer Therapy

Radiotherapy, chemotherapy and hormonal therapy are important components of anticancer therapy. These therapies, especially radiotherapy, may be a strategy for multidisciplinary management of cancer pain.

### Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy has been used to reduce pain and analgesic requirements in symptomatic bone metastasis.

#### Dose-Fractionation Schedules for Palliation

| Schedule | Duration |
|---|---|
| 6-8 Gy / single fraction | 1 day |
| 20 Gy / 5 fractions | 1 week |
| 30 Gy / 10 fractions | 2 weeks |

#### Single vs Multiple Fraction Evidence

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Two meta-analyses | No significant difference in complete and overall response rates for pain control between single and multiple fractions |
| Re-treatment | Significantly higher in single fraction (OR ranging from 2.42 to 2.60) |
| Toxicity | No significant difference between the two arms |

> **Note:** Both meta-analyses failed to mention the quality assessment of the primary papers.

#### SBRT vs Conventional RT

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| SR (4 high-quality RCTs) | SBRT more effective at 3 months (RR=1.41, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.77); no difference at 1 and 6 months |
| Safety | No significant difference between groups |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 14**
> * Radiotherapy may be offered to control pain in symptomatic bone metastasis.
>   * Single-fraction external beam therapy is the preferred choice.

#### Pain Flare-Effect (PFE) Post-Radiotherapy

A transient increase in pain post-radiotherapy. A SR (6 studies) showed corticosteroids were more effective in reducing PFE vs placebo (RR=0.67, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.93).

#### Hemibody Radiotherapy (for Widespread Bone Metastasis)

| Parameter | Result |
|---|---|
| Average time for any pain relief | 3 days |
| Average time for maximum relief | 8 days |
| Haematological grade 3-4 toxicity | Low incidence |

#### Radiotherapy for Advanced Malignancy Pain

**Indications:**
*   Thoracic pain from lung cancer
*   Abdominal and pelvic pain from gynaecological, GI and urological cancers
*   Pain due to locally advanced head and neck cancers

| Outcome | Rate |
|---|---|
| Pain response rates | 67-77% |
| Overall symptomatic response rates | 74-79% |
| Commonly used fractionation | Palliative hypofractionation |

> **Clinical Note:** There is no data on optimal timing for palliative radiotherapy, but it should be considered early in the course of disease.

---

### Other Anticancer Therapy

Other cancer therapies (chemotherapy, hormonal therapy) can reduce pain and improve QoL in patients with chemo-sensitive or hormone-sensitive cancers e.g. breast cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, lymphoma, ovarian cancer and germ cell tumour.

The treatment landscape has changed with the emergence of immunotherapy. However, the role of immunotherapy in cancer pain management is still limited.

#### Immunotherapy vs Other Anticancer Therapies (Retrospective Cohort, PSM Analysis)

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| OMED and NRS in immunotherapy group | Decreased significantly after treatment |
| Comparison with other 3 groups | OMED and NRS were significantly lower in the immunotherapy group after treatment |
| AEs | Fewer AEs in immunotherapy group compared with other three groups |

---

### Radionuclide Therapy

The use of radionuclide therapy for metastatic bone pain, especially in diffuse disease or refractory bone pain, is an option.

#### Evidence (SR — Prostate Cancer Bone Metastases)

| Radiopharmaceutical | Pain Response | Haematological Toxicity (Grade 3-4) |
|---|---|---|
| ¹⁵³Sm | >50-60% | Low incidence |
| ¹⁸⁶Re | >50-60% | Low incidence |
| ¹⁸⁸Re | >50-60% | Low incidence |
| ²²³Ra | >50-60% | Low incidence |

> **Clinical Note:** In view of high cost, limited data and availability, radionuclide and radiation seeds therapy are not a routine option for cancer-related bone pain in this country. No recommendation can be formulated on its use.
