## 4.4.3 Opioid Initiation, Titration and Maintenance

### Initiation

Strong opioids should be initiated at the lowest effective dose. For persistent pain, an IR formulation should be given every four hours to control background pain and with similar doses given up to every hour as needed for breakthrough pain. Patients who have been taking other analgesics, such as NSAIDs, may continue these analgesics after opioid initiation if these agents provide additional analgesia and are not contraindicated.

| Patient Type | Starting Dose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Opioid-naive** | IR oral morphine 5 mg 4-hourly | Safe and effective (p<0.01) |
| **Already on weak opioids** | Morphine at equivalent dose to weak opioid | — |
| **Elderly or frail** | IR oral morphine 2.5 mg 4-6 hourly | Effective (p<0.01) and safe |

> **Definition:** Opioid-naive patients are those not chronically receiving opioid analgesics on a daily basis and therefore have not developed significant tolerance.

---

### Titration

Early assessment and dose titration must be carried out in all patients initiated on opioids. Dose titration can be done as early as 24 hours after initiation.

**When to adjust:**
*   **Dose increment:** Patient reports persistent pain or needs >3 breakthrough doses/day
*   **Dose reduction:** Intolerable side effects present

> **Key Principle:** There is no maximum dose for opioids in cancer pain management. The increase or decrease is usually approximately 25-50% of the total daily dose, taking into consideration patient factors such as organ function, frailty and co-morbidities.

**Options for titration:**

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Method A: Based on rescue doses** | Calculate total daily dose of 4-hourly opioid + breakthrough doses over last 24 hours. Limit to 50% increment within 24 hours. If >50% needed, consult specialist. |
| **Method B: Based on uncontrolled pain** | Increase opioid daily dose by 25-50% for patients who take no rescue doses but still have uncontrolled pain. |

**Example 1 (Method A):**
Patient on aqueous morphine 5 mg 4-hourly, takes 3 extra rescue doses.
*   Total 24h morphine = (5 mg × 6) + (5 mg × 3) = 45 mg/24h
*   New 4-hourly dose: 45 mg ÷ 6 = 7.5 mg (rounded down)
*   **Prescription:** aqueous morphine 7 mg 4-hourly and 7 mg PRN

**Example 2 (Method B):**
Patient on aqueous morphine 5 mg 4-hourly, no rescue doses but uncontrolled pain.
*   Total 24h morphine = (5 mg × 6) = 30 mg/24h
*   NEW 24h morphine = 30 mg + 25% of 30 mg (7.5 mg) = 37.5 mg
*   New 4-hourly dose: 37.5 mg ÷ 6 = 6.25 mg (rounded down)
*   **Prescription:** aqueous morphine 6 mg 4-hourly and 6 mg PRN

---

### Maintenance

Once pain control is adequate and a stable effective dose has been determined, long-acting opioid formulations may be considered for ease of administration.

| Formulation | Dosing Frequency |
|---|---|
| Long-acting morphine or oxycodone (oral) | Every 12 hours |
| Transdermal fentanyl patches | Every 72 hours |

**Example 3 (Conversion to SR):**
Patient's pain control is adequate with aqueous morphine 7.5 mg 4-hourly, no additional breakthrough doses.
*   Total 24h morphine = 7.5 mg × 6 = 45 mg
*   Convert to Tab morphine SR = 45 mg ÷ 2 = 22.5 mg
*   **Prescription:** Tab morphine SR 20* mg BD (morphine SR available in 10 mg & 30 mg tabs)

*\*Rounding of the prescription dose is based on drug strength availability.*

---

### Summary of Morphine Therapy Principles

*   Morphine should be titrated according to individual analgesic response and occurrence of AEs
*   Should be initiated at 5-10 mg 4-hourly using the oral IR formulation
*   Should be started with a lower dose of 2.5-5 mg 4-6 hourly of the IR formulation in the elderly
*   Rapid titration using IV or SC morphine is preferred in patients presenting with severe cancer pain for initial pain control
*   There is no maximum dose for strong opioids in cancer pain management
*   Alternative methods of administration:
    *   4-hourly parenteral morphine/oxycodone
    *   Continuous parenteral opioid infusion
*   Long-term use of opioids must not be abruptly discontinued to avoid withdrawal. Tapering must be conducted in a stepwise fashion, involving patients throughout the process.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 6**
> * Patients with persistent cancer pain should be prescribed with regular (around-the-clock) analgesia.
>   * Opioid doses must be titrated to achieve optimal pain relief with minimal adverse events.
>   * Long-acting opioid formulations may be considered for patients once the effective opioid dose has been established.

## 4.4.4 Breakthrough Pain Management

### Definition and Characteristics

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | An exacerbation of pain in the setting of chronic pain managed with analgesics around-the-clock |
| **Onset** | Typically rapid onset |
| **Intensity** | Severe |
| **Duration** | Self-limiting, average ~30 min |
| **Prevalence** | Affects >50% of patients with cancer |
| **Impact** | May lead to anxiety, depression, decreased functioning and prolonged hospital stays |

### Subtypes

| Subtype | Description | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Spontaneous pain** | Sudden, no identifiable trigger | Rescue analgesia as needed |
| **Incident pain** | Related to activity (e.g. movement), predictable | Pre-emptive medication before triggering activity |
| **End-of-dose failure** | Medication wears off before next scheduled dose | Inadequate dose or interval; adjust ATC medication |

### Rescue Dosing

| Parameter | Guideline |
|---|---|
| **Standard rescue dose** | 5-15% (up to 20%) of MEDD as oral IR opioid |
| **Widely accepted ratio** | 1/6 of 24-hour dose (= 4-hourly opioid dose) |
| **Smaller rescue doses** (e.g. renal impairment) | As low as 1/12 of the 24-hour dose |
| **Frequency** | May be given as required (up to hourly) |
| **ATC adjustment** | Adjust considering total rescue morphine taken in last 24 hours |

A cross-sectional study on patients with advanced cancer reported that 89% of patients with adequately controlled background pain (≤3 on ESAS 0-10) found oral IR opioid to be either effective or very effective in controlling breakthrough pain episodes.

IV opioid titration and bolus administration have also been used to improve control of breakthrough pain.

### Sublingual Fentanyl

Oral transmucosal fentanyl citrate (OTFC), available in Malaysia as sublingual fentanyl, is only indicated for breakthrough cancer pain. Important considerations:

| Consideration | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Eligible patients** | Adults on regular strong opioids (oral morphine ≥60 mg/24h) for ≥1 week |
| **Dose calculation** | Total OTFC dose taken **cannot** be used to calculate new ATC dose |
| **Interchangeability** | Not interchangeable with other IR opioids |
| **Non-inferiority vs SC morphine** | FST 100 mcg did not demonstrate non-inferiority to SC morphine 5 mg (RD= -13%, not significant) |
| **Comparison with oral morphine** | Mean pain intensity significantly lower with FST at day 3, 7, 15 and 30; faster relief and shorter titration |

*   Rescue dose for breakthrough pain is given as often as required (up to hourly).
*   ATC dose is adjusted considering the total amount of rescue dose for the last 24 hours.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 7**
> * All patients with cancer pain who are on opioids should be prescribed with rescue analgesia if required to ensure optimal pain control.
> * Opioids (morphine or oxycodone) for breakthrough cancer pain should be prescribed at 1/6 to 1/12 of the 24-hour dose.

## 4.4.5 Opioid Rotation

Opioid rotation is a strategy of switching from one opioid to another to improve pain relief or reduce AEs. This strategy may be indicated in up to 44% of patients with cancer-related pain. Improvement in pain as well as reduced AEs after rotation were seen in 50-90% of these patients.

### Common Indications

| Indication | Setting |
|---|---|
| Inadequate pain relief despite appropriate titration | Outpatient (most common reason: uncontrolled pain) |
| Intolerable AEs (e.g. sedation, nausea, vomiting, constipation) | Inpatient (most common reason: AEs) |
| Organ impairment | Both |
| Practical considerations (e.g. lack of compliance, inability to swallow) | Both |

### Evidence (Systematic Review)

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Pain improvement | All studies showed pain improvement or stable pain relief with opioid rotation |
| Dose titration | May still be necessary to achieve stable analgesia |
| Opioid superiority | No particular opioid demonstrated superiority to another |
| Higher first-line dose | Tended to result in lower success rates of rotation |
| AE reduction | Limited with rotation |
| Patient satisfaction | Generally positive, ranging from 60-90% |

### Dose Conversion Guidance

> **Key Principle:** Reduce the calculated dose by 25-50% when initiating opioid rotation and titrate upwards accordingly. Conversion must always consider co-morbidities, concomitant medications, pain/AE intensity and pharmacokinetic factors.

**Reported conversion ratios (systematic review):**
*   Morphine-oxycodone: **1.5:1**
*   Oral morphine-transdermal fentanyl: **100:1**
*   Different opioids to methadone: highly variable, ranging from **5:1 to 10:1**

### Instructions for Using the Conversion Table

1. This conversion chart should only be used as a guide and treatment must be individually tailored for patients based on clinical assessment.
2. When changing from one opioid to another, consider a dose reduction of 25-50% due to incomplete cross-tolerance.
3. Consider reduced doses in the elderly and in patients with renal or significant hepatic impairment.
4. Calculate the total 24-hour opioid dose in mg (for fentanyl, note that the hourly rate is in mcg).
5. Begin at the left-hand column and identify the opioid currently used.
6. Select the alternative opioid from the top row.
7. Identify the box where the column and row intersect and determine the conversion factor to divide or multiply in order to obtain the 24-hour dose of the alternative opioid.
8. Divide 24-hour dose according to the dosing frequency required (e.g. divide by 2 for BD dosing and divide by 6 for 4-hourly dosing).
9. Calculate the rescue dose for breakthrough pain for each opioid as approximately 1/6 to 1/12 of the total daily dose.

### Additional Conversions

| From | To | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| PO dihydrocodeine 90 mg/day | PO morphine | 10-12 mg/day |
| PO tramadol 150 mg/day | PO morphine | 15-30 mg/day |
| TD fentanyl 25 mcg/hour | Continuous SC/IV infusion fentanyl | 25 mcg/hour |
| SC morphine | IV morphine | Equivalent (1:1) |

### Conversion Examples

**Example 1: Oral morphine → oral oxycodone**
*   Oral morphine mg/day (20 mg 4-hourly = 120 mg per day)
*   Conversion factor = divide by 1.5
*   Equivalent dose of oxycodone = 120 ÷ 1.5 = 80 mg per day
*   Reduce equivalent dose by 25% = 60 mg per day (due to incomplete cross-tolerance)
*   **Therefore:** dose of CR oxycodone = 30 mg twice daily

**Example 2: Oral morphine → transdermal fentanyl**
*   Oral morphine mg/day (16 mg 4-hourly = 100 mg per day)
*   Conversion factor = divide by 3
*   Equivalent dose of transdermal fentanyl = 100 ÷ 3 = 33 mcg per hour
*   Reduce equivalent dose by 25% = 25 mcg per hour (due to incomplete cross-tolerance)
*   **Therefore:** dose of TD fentanyl = 25 mcg per hour

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 8**
> * Opioid rotation should be considered in patients with cancer pain who are not responding to dose escalation or experiencing intolerable adverse events.

## 4.4.6 Opioids Requiring Special Attention

### Methadone

Methadone is an alternative treatment in specialist services for special circumstances e.g. difficult pain, renal impairment, neuropathic pain syndrome and hyperalgesic states. In Malaysia, methadone is mainly used for harm reduction in Methadone Replacement Therapy. However, its method of use is different in treating cancer pain.

| Evidence Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Cochrane SR (6 studies) | Methadone was similar to morphine in effectiveness of pain control | Low to very low (GRADE) |
| Cochrane SR (tolerability) | Somnolence more common with methadone; dry mouth more common with morphine | Low to very low (GRADE) |
| SR (10 small studies) | Methadone effective and safe as first-line analgesic | No quality assessment mentioned |

> **WHO Guidance:** Due to complex nature and wide inter-individual variation in pharmacokinetics, methadone should be initiated only by practitioners experienced in cancer pain.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 9**
> * Methadone may be considered in the management of cancer pain
>   * It should only be prescribed by healthcare providers experienced with its use in the management of cancer pain.

---

### Pethidine

**Pethidine should NOT be used in chronic cancer pain management.**

| Risk | Detail |
|---|---|
| Toxic metabolite | Norpethidine accumulation with long-term or high-dose use |
| Seizure risk | Norpethidine can cause seizures |
| Addiction risk | Increased risk of addiction |
| Euphoria | Associated with higher incidence of euphoria |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 10**
> * Pethidine should not be used in the management of cancer pain.

## 4.4.7 Opioids in Special Populations

### Renal and Liver Impairment

Renal and liver impairment alter the pharmacokinetics of many medications including opioids by changing opioid metabolism and reducing its clearance, resulting in accumulation of the opioid metabolites. These generally result in more AEs and an increased risk of toxicity.

#### Morphine in Renal Impairment

| Metabolite | Activity | Concern in Renal Impairment |
|---|---|---|
| **Morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G)** | Inactive | Accumulates in renal impairment |
| **Morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G)** | Active | Accumulates; associated with CNS AEs and respiratory depression |

**Evidence (SR of 18 studies):**

| Finding | Odds Ratio |
|---|---|
| Severe constipation in renal impairment (<90 ml/min/1.73 m²) vs normal renal function | OR=1.91 (95% CI 1.08 to 3.37) |
| Severe cognitive dysfunction with higher serum morphine concentrations | OR=1.77 (95% CI 1.13 to 2.78) |
| Predictors of morphine intolerance | Age >78 years, high WCC, high platelet count on concomitant poor liver or renal function |

#### Oxycodone in Renal Impairment

Elimination of oxycodone and its metabolites was significantly prolonged in renal impairment. Patients with increased serum concentrations were more likely to report severe fatigue (OR=1.70, 95% CI 1.04 to 2.78).

#### Fentanyl in Renal Impairment

Fentanyl is metabolised in the liver and its metabolites have minimal or no pharmacological effect. It is **least likely to cause harm** when used appropriately.

> **Note:** Another SR (15 studies) on opioids for cancer patients with moderate to severe cancer pain and renal impairment found no studies able to identify the risk of toxicity. There was also no direct clinical evidence on the level of impairment where caution is needed.

#### Recommendations for eGFR 30-89 ml/min (Mild to Moderate Renal Impairment)

*   Assess for any reversible factors
*   All opioids appropriate for cancer pain can be used with consideration of a reduced dose or frequency
*   Monitor for changes in renal function and consider opioid switching in rapidly deteriorating renal function
*   Be aware that estimations of GFR may be less accurate in the presence of cachexia, low protein states, oedema and acute renal failure; a lower eGFR should prompt consideration of a change of opioid to one considered safer in renal impairment

#### Hepatic Impairment

In a SR (3 studies) assessing opioid use in cancer patients with hepatic impairment, there was an increase in morphine and M6G concentrations although this was not significant. Therefore, there was a need for dose adjustment for morphine and oxycodone. The available evidence was heterogeneous and of low quality.

> **Previous MoH CPG Guidance:** All opioids should be used cautiously and at reduced doses and/or reduced frequency in patients with renal and/or liver impairment.

---

### Older Adults

There is paucity of data on the use of opioids in older adults with cancer pain.

| Challenge | Impact |
|---|---|
| Multiple co-morbidities | Greater vulnerability to AEs |
| Cognitive impairment (e.g. dementia) | Difficulty in pain assessment |
| Increasing frailty / loss of physiologic reserve | Decreased capacity to deal with pain and treatment |
| Polypharmacy | Reduced available treatment options |

> **Key Principle:** In older adults with cancer pain, start medication at a low dose and titrate slowly.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 11**
> * In the management of cancer pain for older patients or those with renal/liver impairment:
>   * All opioids should be used with caution.* (*Fentanyl is a safer opioid in renal impairment.)
>   * Adjustment in doses/frequency of opioids should be considered.

## 4.4.8 Opioid Side Effects

Opioids are generally well-tolerated and safe in cancer pain management. In a large SR (25 studies), nausea and constipation were most common, whilst vomiting, drowsiness and dry mouth were less frequent. There was a dose-effect relationship, with higher AE rates seen at higher opioid starting doses and after titration, particularly with morphine.

### Side Effect Details

| Side Effect | Incidence | Key Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Constipation** | 25% | Commonest AE; prophylactic laxatives recommended for all patients on regular opioid therapy. Lower rate with fentanyl than morphine. |
| **Nausea** | 21% | Temporary; tolerance develops in 5-10 days. Treat with anti-emetics (metoclopramide, haloperidol, prochlorperazine). |
| **Vomiting** | 13% | Temporary; tolerance develops in 5-10 days. Treat with anti-emetics. |
| **Sedation/Drowsiness** | 13% (somnolence); up to 88% (drowsiness) | Occurs at initiation, tends to resolve within a week. Drowsiness higher with oxycodone even at low doses. |
| **Dry mouth** | 17-94% | Rated moderate to severe by patients. Non-pharmacological measures: oral hygiene, sugar-free gum, saliva stimulant spray/gel. |
| **Delirium/Neurotoxicity** | — | Transient mild cognitive impairment may occur upon initiation; usually resolves in 1-2 weeks. |

### Table 5. Management of Opioid Side Effects

| Side Effects | Management |
|---|---|
| **Constipation** | Faecal softeners (e.g. lactulose, macrogol), Stimulant laxatives (e.g. bisacodyl, senna) |
| **Nausea and Vomiting** | Anti-emetics (e.g. Metoclopramide, Haloperidol, Prochlorperazine) |
| **Dry Mouth** | Non-pharmacological treatment: Good oral hygiene, Sugar-free chewing gum and candies/sweets, Saliva stimulants (e.g. mouth spray/gel) |
| **Sedation and drowsiness** | Opioid dose reduction, titrate to lowest effective dose, consider opioid switching. Methylphenidate and other psychostimulant drugs can be considered if necessary. |
| **Delirium and neurotoxicity (e.g. confusion and myoclonus)** | Dose reduction and opioid switching. Clonazepam, sodium valproate and baclofen for myoclonus. Antipsychotics for delirium. |
| **Pruritus** | Antihistamines. Opioid switching if symptoms are severe. |

> **Recommendation 12**
> * Opioid-induced side effects should be proactively identified and treated adequately to ensure optimum cancer pain management.
> * Laxatives should be prophylactically prescribed in patients with cancer pain and on regular opioid therapy.

## 4.4.9 Opioid Toxicity

### Respiratory Depression

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Occurrence** | Very rare; may occur during rapid titration. Uncommon during chronic administration. |
| **Safety principle** | When appropriately titrated against patient's pain, strong opioids do not cause clinically important respiratory depression. |
| **Early indicator** | Sedation almost always precedes respiratory depression. Sedation assessment is a good early clinical indicator. |

**If severe respiratory depression occurs (RR <8/min):**
*   Administer very low doses of naloxone: 40 mcg (0.04 mg)
*   Titrate every 1-3 minutes against patient's respiratory rate
*   **Do NOT give large bolus doses** — this reverses analgesic effects and causes major physical withdrawal syndromes
*   Severe hypertension, pulmonary oedema, cardiac arrhythmia and cardiac arrest have been reported with naloxone use

> **Cross-reference from Appendix 7:**
> **GUIDE FOR NALOXONE USE**
> 
> **General Principles:**
> * Naloxone, a specific opioid antagonist, is seldom necessary in the palliative care setting when opioids are appropriately titrated against the patient's pain.
> * It is indicated for the reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression and not for treating drowsiness and/or delirium associated with opioids.
> * The dose administered should be carefully titrated against level of consciousness and satisfactory respiratory function (≥8 breaths/minute and no cyanosis).
> * Titration is important to avoid acute withdrawal syndrome and severe pain.
> 
> **Pharmacokinetics & Availability:**
> * Route of administration: IV is preferable, but SC or IM can also be used
> * Onset of action: 1-2 minutes (IV) and 2-5 minutes (SC/IM)
> * Half-life: approximately 1 hour
> * Pack size: 1 ampoule = 400 mcg/1 ml
> * Adverse effects (usually with large bolus doses): abdominal cramps, nausea and vomiting, flushing, arrhythmias and erythema at injection site
> 
> **Treatment:**
> * Respiratory depression is usually preceded by a progressive reduction in consciousness.
> * If the respiratory rate ≥8 breaths/minute and patient can be easily aroused (e.g. opens eyes to verbal command), monitor patient closely and consider omitting or reducing the dose of the regular opioid.
> * If respiratory rate is ≤8 breaths/min and patient is unresponsive, discontinue the ongoing opioid (e.g. stop CSCI/CIVI, remove TD patch) and naloxone should be administered.
>   * Dilute 1 ampoule (400 mcg) of naloxone in 10 ml water for injection.
>   * Administer small boluses of 0.5 ml (20 mcg) every two minutes until respiratory rate is satisfactory, and patient is easily arousable (need not be fully alert).
>   * After the last dose of naloxone, continue to monitor the patient.
>   * Further boluses of naloxone might be necessary because naloxone is shorter acting than the opioids.
>   * A naloxone infusion may be considered if recovery is not satisfactory with multiple bolus doses.
> * After patient recovers, the regular opioid regimen must be reviewed to consider possible causes for the respiratory depression (e.g. drug interactions, drug accumulation due to renal impairment, medication errors) and necessary modifications made to the regimen.
> 
> **Additional Cautions:**
> * Do not use large bolus doses e.g. '1 ampoule stat' in patients who are receiving opioids for chronic pain relief.
> * Pupil size is an unreliable indicator of opioid overdose in patients taking regular opioids.
> * Naloxone should not be given to patients on opioids when death is expected and imminent; a slow respiratory rate is a normal occurrence.

## 4.4.10 Tolerance and Addiction to Opioids

### Tolerance to Opioids

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | Long-term body adaptation to opioids resulting in reduced clinical effectiveness with repeated use at the same dose |
| **Mechanism** | Contributed by downregulation of mu-receptors in neuronal cells; exact mechanism not well understood |
| **Contributing factors** | Persistent pain, chronic opioid administration, reduced expression of opioid receptors on certain cancer cells |

> **Key Principle:** Fear of opioid tolerance should not cause any hesitation to start or increase opioid therapy for cancer patients experiencing pain.

**Referral thresholds:**

| Opioid | Dose Threshold for Specialist Referral |
|---|---|
| Oral morphine | >600 mg/day |
| Oral oxycodone | >400 mg/day |
| Transdermal fentanyl | >200 mcg/hour |

---

### Addiction/Misuse of Opioids

The incidence of opioid misuse in advanced cancer patients differs widely. Among children, adolescents and young adults, misuse behaviours ranged from 7% to 90%. Nonspecific substance use disorders ranged between 2% to 35% of adults with cancer.

**Risk Factors for Opioid Misuse or Abuse:**
*   History of dependence or misuse of prescription drugs, illicit drugs or alcohol prior to cancer diagnosis/treatment
*   History of binge drinking (alcohol) or peers who binge drink
*   Family history of substance abuse
*   History of psychiatric disorder (anxiety, depression, ADHD, PTSD, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia)
*   History of sexual abuse victimisation
*   Young age (<45 years old)
*   History of legal problems or incarceration

### Screening Tools

| Tool | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **SOAPP-R** (Screener and Opioid Assessment for Patients with Pain-Revised) | Predicting opioid misuse in patients considered for long-term opioid therapy |
| **ORT** (Opioid Risk Tool) | Predicting opioid misuse in patients considered for long-term opioid therapy |
| **COMM** (Current Opioid Misuse Measure) | Detecting aberrant behaviour in patients already on opioids |

*   It is important to identify patients at risk of opioid misuse so that they can be closely monitored.
*   Fear of opioid misuse or tolerance should not preclude the start of opioid therapy for cancer patients experiencing pain.
