## Overview

| Technique | Primary Indication |
|---|---|
| **Neurolysis** | Refractory visceral/pelvic/perineal cancer pain |
| **Neuraxial opioids** | Refractory cancer pain not controlled by systemic treatment |
| **Vertebroplasty** | Pain from malignant spinal compression fractures |
| **Surgical intervention** | Large painful tumours, malignant bowel obstruction, pathologic fractures |

## 6.1 Neurolysis

Neurolysis is performed by specialists trained in interventional pain management. It requires instilling a chemical ablative solution (e.g. alcohol or phenol with local anaesthetics) or physical ablation (e.g. surgical resection and radiofrequency denervation) into the nerve plexus resulting in nerve destruction or degeneration.

**Common targets:**
*   Coeliac plexus or ganglia
*   Splanchnic plexus
*   Superior hypogastric plexus (SHG)
*   Walther's ganglia or ganglion impar

### Coeliac Plexus Neurolysis (CPN) — Evidence

| Source | Finding | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Cochrane SR (6 RCTs, advanced pancreatic cancer) | CPN more effective at reducing pain vs standard analgesic therapy at 4 weeks (MD= -0.42, 95% CI -0.70 to -0.13) and 8 weeks (MD= -0.44, 95% CI -0.89 to -0.01) | Moderate (based on 3 bias domains) |
| Meta-analysis (10 RCTs, unresectable pancreatic cancer) | Pain control achieved at 4 weeks via percutaneous, intraoperative or endoscopic approaches vs standard medical management (MD= -0.58, 95% CI -1.09 to -0.07) | Moderate (GRADE) |
| Meta-analysis (EUS-guided CPN) | Response rate of 46% (95% CI 36 to 55) using central injection technique | — |

#### CPN Adverse Effects

| AE | Incidence |
|---|---|
| Transient hypotension | 20-41.7% |
| Inebriation | 6.9-12.5% |
| Diarrhoea | 0-25% |
| Burning pain at injection site | 6.9-10% |
| Nausea | 8.3% |
| Spinal stroke (major, EUS-guided) | 0.2% (rare) |
| Visceral ischaemia (major, EUS-guided) | Even more rare |

---

### Superior Hypogastric Plexus (SHG) Neurolysis — Evidence

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| 10-year retrospective cohort (pelvic cancer pain) | Significant pain score reduction in 59.4% (1 month), 55.5% (3 months), 48.8% (6 months) |
| Same study (AEs) | Transient hypotension (5.56%), transient urinary incontinence (0.56%), iliac artery puncture (0.56%), hypertension (0.56%) |
| Same study (re-treatment) | 5.5% of patients at 3 months to 1 year follow-up |

#### SHG + Pulsed Radiofrequency of Sacral Roots

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| RCT (perineal and pelvic cancer pain) | SHG + pulsed RF of sacral roots showed better analgesic response vs SHG alone up to 3 months (MD= -0.67, 95% CI -1.29 to -0.05) |

---

### Ganglion Impar Neurolysis — Evidence

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Pre- and post-study (uncontrolled pelvic oncologic pain) | Significant reduction in pain score and morphine consumption up to 3 months follow-up |

> **Note:** Evidence on somatic plexus neurolysis (e.g. brachial or lumbosacral plexus) was confined to case reports. AEs were not discussed in the SHG + RF RCT or the ganglion impar study.

## 6.2 Neuraxial Opioids

Neuraxial opioids involve the delivery of drugs via epidural, intrathecal or intracerebroventricular routes. A catheter drug delivery system (subcutaneous implanted device or spinal port with external syringe pump) provides an effective therapeutic option for refractory cancer pain.

### Evidence Summary

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Cohort (refractory pancreatic cancer pain) | 64.3% with intrathecal drug delivery systems experienced >50% pain reduction from baseline after 3 months (p<0.01) |
| EPCRC SR | No difference in pain scores between neuraxial bolus and continuous opioid infusion. No difference between epidural morphine and systemic morphine. Spinal opioid therapy may be effective (weak evidence). |
| Recent meta-analysis (retrospective studies) | Significant mean pain score reduction of 3.64 (95% CI 3.09 to 4.18) up to 1 month post-implantation. Improvements in symptom severity associated with improved QoL. All studies showed systemic opioid dose reduction following implantation. |

**Most common intrathecal opioid:** Morphine (alone or in combination with adjuvants such as bupivacaine, ropivacaine, clonidine or baclofen).

### SAEs from Intrathecal Implantable Devices (Product Surveillance Registry)

| SAE | Incidence |
|---|---|
| Infection | 3.2% |
| Postdural puncture headaches / CSF leaks | 1.27% |
| Pump pocket haematoma | 0.28% |
| Pneumonia | 0.14% |

> **Note:** Other AEs of systemic opioids may also occur with neuraxial therapy e.g. nausea, pruritus, urinary retention, constipation, respiratory depression, sedation and confusion.

## 6.3 Vertebroplasty

Vertebroplasty is a percutaneous vertebral augmentation procedure that requires injection of cement into cancellous bone of the vertebral body. It relieves pain due to spinal compression fractures caused by osteoporosis or malignant infiltration.

**Key features:**
*   Minimally invasive and relatively safe
*   Increases stability of the spine by preventing vertebral body collapse
*   Suitable for patients who do not respond to conservative therapy or are poor candidates for open surgery

### Evidence (SR of 7 RCTs, Low Risk of Bias)

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Percutaneous cement vertebroplasty or balloon kyphoplasty (alone or with local therapies) significantly effective for cancer pain from malignant spinal compression fractures |
| Local therapies combined | Iodine-125 seeds, chemotherapy, radiofrequency ablation or corticosteroids |
| Most common AE | Cement leakage at 24% (95% CI 11 to 44) |
| Morbidity/mortality | No significant morbidity or mortality reported |

## 6.4 Surgical Intervention

Surgery has a role in the management of cancer pain. The decision should be made by a multidisciplinary team considering other treatment options, risks and expectations of patient and family members.

### Indications and Evidence

| Intervention | Indication | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Ablative surgery** | Large painful tumours (e.g. fungating breast lesions, sarcomas) | May improve pain control where analgesics and other interventions provide suboptimal relief |
| **Palliative surgery** (colostomy, bypass) | Malignant bowel obstruction | Relieves pain from obstruction |
| **Cordotomy** | Mesothelioma-related pain | SR: might be effective and safe, but evidence limited in quantity and quality |
| **Orthopaedic oncology surgery** | Metastatic long bone disease | Multicentre cohort: significantly improved functional outcome and pain as early as 2 weeks post-op. Should be considered for impending or pathologic fracture in patients with expected short-term survival. |

---

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Recommendation 16**
> * Patients whose pain control is poor despite optimal pharmacological therapy should be referred to specialists trained in interventional pain management for consideration of the following interventions:
>   * coeliac plexus neurolysis for advanced pancreatic cancer pain
>   * superior hypogastric plexus or ganglion impar neurolysis for advanced pelvic and perineal cancer pain
>   * intrathecal drug delivery system
>   * vertebroplasty for malignant spinal compression fractures
