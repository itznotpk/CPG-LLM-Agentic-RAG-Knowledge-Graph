## 5.1: Prevalence Of Childhood And Adolescent Obesity

Findings from the **NHMS 2019** showed:
- 29.8% of children aged 5–17 years were either overweight or obese (of which 15.0% were adolescents)
- Prevalence of obesity alone has steadily risen from **3.9% in 2011 to 14.8% in 2019**

Childhood overweight and obesity is a significant public health concern given adverse effects on health and quality of life in childhood, as well as increased risk of obesity and associated health complications in adulthood.

## 5.2: Diagnosis Of Childhood And Adolescent Obesity

BMI is the accepted standard for determining overweight and obesity prevalence in children and adolescents. As BMI changes with age, **BMI-for-age percentiles** are used in clinical settings.

An early BMI rebound before 5 years of age carries a risk for adult obesity.

Diagnosis should be based on **BMI and the WHO 2007 reference system:**

**Table 5-1. Diagnosis Of Overweight And Obesity In Children And Adolescents**

| Age (years) | Overweight | Obese |
|---|---|---|
| 5–19 | BMI >1 SD above the WHO Growth Reference median | BMI >2 SD above the WHO Growth Reference median |
| Under-5 | BMI >2 SD above the WHO Child Growth Standards median | BMI >3 SD above the WHO Child Growth Standards median |

In cases of extreme obesity, the US CDC normative BMI percentiles can also be used — extreme obesity is defined as BMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile or BMI ≥35 kg/m².

> **[Grade C]** BMI cut-offs using the WHO BMI-for-age chart should be used for the classification of overweight and obesity in children.

## 5.3: Clinical Evaluation

Objectives of clinical evaluation:
- Determine any pathological causes of obesity
- Assess lifestyle and eating habits
- Ascertain the presence of obesity-related comorbidities

### 5.3.1: Causes Of Obesity — Primary vs Secondary

**Primary obesity** (>95% of cases): Multiple common and rare genetic variants contribute along with obvious environmental drivers. Children with primary obesity usually have accelerated growth, risk of early secondary sexual characteristics, and may have advanced bone age.

**Secondary obesity** (uncommon): Should be carefully considered:
- Endocrinopathies: Cushing's syndrome, hypothyroidism, hypopituitarism
- Syndromic/genetic conditions: pseudohypoparathyroidism, Prader-Willi syndrome

Children with endocrinopathies may have decreased linear growth or short stature. Clinicians should refer maturing children with obesity, short stature, and decreased growth velocity despite continued weight gain for further evaluation.

Genetic obesity syndromes — features to identify:
- Short stature; dysmorphic facies; retinitis pigmentosa; deafness; polydactyly; hyperphagia; consanguinity
- **With developmental delay:** Prader-Willi Syndrome, Albright hereditary osteodystrophy, Bardet-Biedl syndrome
- **Without developmental delay:** Alstrom syndrome, POMC deficiency, MC4R deficiency

### 5.3.2: Patient History

Key areas to assess:
- **Developmental:** Type of delivery, birth weight/length, gestational age, maternal GDM; infant feeding (duration of breastfeeding); growth and development; academic performance
- **Physical and mental health:** Weight history; medications contributing to weight gain; symptoms of hyperglycaemia (polyuria/polydipsia), OSA (habitual snoring, daytime sleepiness, morning headaches), and PCOS (acne, hirsutism, menstrual irregularity)
- **Health behaviours:** Dietary intake (sugar-containing drinks, fruit/vegetable intake); dietary patterns; physical activity and sedentary time; screen time; family capacity for behavioural change
- **Mental health:** Social isolation; risk of bullying; persistent anxiety, depression or self-harm; eating disorders

### 5.3.3: Physical Examination

[Level 2+/3] Clinicians should examine:
1. Weight, height, and BMI
2. Waist circumference (WC)
3. Blood pressure using height-age-sex percentile normalised BP tables
4. Acanthosis nigricans and skin tags
5. Dysmorphic features (Prader-Willi, Albright Osteodystrophy, Alstrom Syndrome)
6. Fundoscopic examination
7. Thyroid examination for goitre
8. Tanner staging/extreme acne and hirsutism in pubertal girls
9. Abdominal examination for hepatomegaly
10. Musculoskeletal examination of lower limbs (Blount's disease)

### 5.3.4: Investigations For Comorbidities

[Level 4] Criteria for screening for comorbidities:
1. Children and adolescents **with obesity** should be offered screening for related comorbidities
2. **Overweight** children and adolescents should be offered screening if ≥2 of the following risk factors are present:
   - Features of insulin resistance (hypertension, acanthosis nigricans)
   - Strong family history of T2DM, hypertension, or premature coronary heart disease (1st or 2nd degree relatives)
   - History of maternal GDM
   - Birth history of SGA or LGA
   - Signs/symptoms of comorbidities (hyperglycaemia, OSA, hirsutism/PCOS)
3. Start screening at **ages ≥10 years or at onset of puberty**, whichever comes first (screen earlier if symptoms or rapid BMI increase present)

**Table 5-3. Screening For Comorbidities Of Paediatric Overweight/Obesity**

| Comorbidity | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Prehypertension/hypertension | Diagnosis based on 3 separate measurements at least 1 week apart (refer to BP tables by age/height percentile) |
| Dyslipidaemia | Fasting lipid profile (typically high TG and low HDL-C in obesity) |
| Prediabetes/diabetes mellitus | Fasting plasma glucose or OGTT with glucose 1.75 g/kg (maximum 75 g) |
| Microalbuminuria | Urine microalbumin |
| MAFLD | Liver function test; ultrasound of hepatobiliary system if transaminitis present |
| Sleep disorders | Referral for evaluation of OSA (e.g., polysomnography); varied presentations including snoring, daytime sleepiness, hyperactivity |
| PCOS/menstrual irregularities | Testosterone and SHBG; pelvic ultrasound if indicated |

> **[Grade C]** Clinical evaluation (history and physical examination) should be performed to identify the aetiology and to rule out pathological causes.
> **[Grade C]** Children and adolescents with obesity should be offered screening for related comorbidities.
> **[Grade C]** Overweight children and adolescents should be offered screening if risk factors are present.

## 5.4: Management Of Obesity In Children And Adolescents

Management focuses on changes in health behaviours (dietary and physical activity) rather than actual weight loss alone. The approach depends on severity of obesity, age, developmental stage, and child/family preferences.

### 5.4.1: Basic Management Principles By Age

**Table 5-4. Basic Management Principles By Age**

| Age | Basic Management |
|---|---|
| Infants | Exclusive breastfeeding; delay complementary food until 6 months; no sugar sweetened beverages; no screen time |
| Toddlers (2–4 years) | Parents model healthy behaviour; three main meals with 1–2 snacks; encourage routine sleep pattern |
| Children (5–9 years) | Parents as role models; involve family; minimise recreational screen time; daily activity as vigorously as possible |
| Older children/Adolescents | Peer group support; regular sleep time; technology to track exercise and meal intake |

### 5.4.2: Family Involvement

Family involvement is crucial for managing obesity in children and adolescents.

[Level 1++] Intensive lifestyle modification programmes can be effective tools for paediatric weight control. [Level 1++] Family-based interventions with parental involvement have improved outcomes.

[Level 1+] When providing interventions, it is also necessary to focus on **family health behaviours**. The level of family involvement among adolescents will depend on their age and maturity.

[Level 1++] Following the treatment phase, a formal **maintenance programme** should be continued to maintain achieved weight loss.

### 5.4.3: Staged Management Approach

[Level 4] A step or staged approach consisting of 3 stages is recommended:

**Table 5-5. Staged Approach To Obesity Management In Children And Adolescents**

| Stage | Components | Implementation | Visit Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Develop structured plan with patient and family; log book for monitoring (parental supervision). Diet: Address eating behaviours; ≥5 servings fruits and vegetables/day; eliminate sugar-sweetened beverages. Physical activity: 60 min/day average; reduce recreational screen time to <2 hours/day | Primary care | 3–6 monthly |
| **Stage 2** | Continue diet and physical activity; multidisciplinary approach (dietitian, physical activity counsellor, psychological services); medical screening for complications | Obesity clinic/Paediatrician | 3 monthly |
| **Stage 3** | Stages 1 and 2 components; increase intensity; consider medical therapy or referral for bariatric surgery | Tertiary weight management centre/Paediatric endocrinologist | More frequent |

Start at Stage 1, 2 or 3 depending on age, degree of obesity, health risks, and motivation. Advance if failure to achieve or maintain goal BMI.

**Refer for specialist care if:**
- Patient is <5 years old
- Clinical suspicion of underlying medical/genetic syndrome or endocrine cause
- Comorbidities requiring weight management (OSA, metabolic complications)

### Dietary Recommendations For Children

[Level 1++] Clinicians should advocate for healthy eating habits:
- Consumption of 5 servings of vegetables and fruits
- Start each day with a healthy breakfast
- Decrease consumption of fast foods and processed foods
- Water as the beverage of choice
- Reduce saturated dietary fat intake
- Portion control education
- Timely regular meals to avoid constant grazing
- Eat meals as a family whenever possible
- Separate eating from other activities (TV, computer)
- Have healthy foods readily available
- Avoid using foods as treats/rewards
- Comfort children with attention, listening, and affection instead of food

[Level 1++] A meta-analysis found that Very Low-Calorie Diets (VLCD) are effective at inducing rapid short-term weight loss in children and adolescents — however, it lacks long-term data.

### Physical Activity For Children

[Level 1+] Regular physical activity can improve weight loss and maintenance and reduce CV risk factors even without weight loss.

[Level 1+] Children/adolescents should spend on average **60 minutes/day on moderate or vigorous physical activity**. The increase can be achieved starting from 2–3 years old by active play.

[Level 1+] Children should engage in **resistance training activities at least 3 times a week** (supervised). [Level 1+] Combined aerobic and strength training ranked best in reducing BMI Z-scores.

[Level 1+] Classic sports activities have beneficial effects on overall physical activity level, body composition, and metabolic profile.

### Sleep Recommendations For Children

[Level 1++] Various studies demonstrate a link between obesity and reduced sleep duration. Electronic devices should be removed from the bedroom.

**Table 5-6. Recommended Hours Of Sleep Based On Age**

| Age (years) | Hours of Sleep |
|---|---|
| 3–5 | 10–13 |
| 6–13 | 9–11 |
| 14–17 | 8–10 |

### Screen Time

[Level 1+] Screen times ≥2 hours/day demonstrated increased risk of overweight/obesity among children.

[Level 1++] Recreational screen time should be limited to:
- **No more than 1 hour/day** for 2–5-year-olds
- **No more than 2 hours/day** for children 6 years and older

### Psychosocial Aspects

[Level 2+] Maladaptive rearing habits around diet and exercise must be identified. Parenting styles contribute to obesity in children.

[Level 2+] Self-esteem becomes closely tied to body image during adolescence. [Level 1+] Significant association between eating disorders and youth who are overweight/obese.

Mental health screening — ask about:
- School absences or refusal
- Teasing by peers regarding weight or appearance
- Persistent symptoms of anxiety, depression, or self-harm
- Eating disorders (anorexia or binge eating)
- Family dynamics and attitudes about weight

### Medications (Adolescents Only)

[Level 1+] The only two medications approved by the USFDA are **liraglutide** and **orlistat**.

Pharmacotherapy: [Level 1++; Level 4]
- Can be considered **only after** a formal programme of intensive lifestyle modification has proven suboptimal or unsuccessful
- Should be administered **only with a concomitant lifestyle modification programme** in a specialised centre
- Needs to be re-evaluated if there is failure of BMI reduction after 12 weeks (<4% BMI/BMI z-score reduction)

**Liraglutide:** [Level 1++] Approved for chronic obesity treatment in adolescents aged **12–18 years** (USFDA and EMA). GLP1-RA; reduces appetite, slows gastric motility, and enhances satiety. In the largest liraglutide RCT, 3 mg daily resulted in a mean placebo-subtracted BMI reduction of approximately **5% at one year**. Most common adverse event: gastrointestinal.

**Orlistat:** [Level 1++] Approved for obesity treatment in those above **12 years of age**. Inhibits GI lipases, reducing fat absorption by ~30%. Significantly reduces BMI in adolescents, but associated with significant gastrointestinal side effects.

**Metformin:** Not approved for obesity management, but may be useful in those with PCOS.

### Bariatric Surgery (Adolescents)

[Level 1+] Metabolic and bariatric surgery can be considered in adolescents who have severe obesity and significant comorbidity, prior to surgical referral the patient should demonstrate ability to adhere to healthy dietary/activity habits and compliance with formal lifestyle modification.

[Level 1+] Data from Teen-LABS and AMOS studies have shown good outcomes in reduction of BMI and cardiometabolic effects (remission of diabetes and dyslipidaemia) in adolescents.

[Level 1+] Both **Vertical Sleeve Gastrectomy (VSG) and Roux-En-Y Gastric Bypass (RYGB)** are safe and effective procedures in adolescents; however, there is still a lack of significant long-term data. VSG had lower risk of complications.

Most common complications: abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nausea, or dehydration; stricture with RYGB; wound infection with VSG.

> **[Grade D]** A step or staged approach for weight management in the paediatric population should be used.
> - For prepubertal children: allow a **gradual decline in BMI** with weight maintenance or slower weight gain.
> - For pubertal children: gradual weight loss with a **maximum loss of 0.5–1.0 kg per month**.
>
> **[Grade C]** Family-focused lifestyle intervention should be emphasised.
>
> **[Grade B]** Treatment programmes should target: decreasing overall dietary intake; increasing physical activity; decreasing sedentary behaviour; addressing sleep behaviour.
>
> **[Grade B]** Pharmacotherapy may be considered in adolescents if formal intensive lifestyle modification programmes fail. Liraglutide may be considered.
>
> **[Grade B]** Metabolic and bariatric surgery may be considered for adolescents with extreme obesity and significant comorbidities.
