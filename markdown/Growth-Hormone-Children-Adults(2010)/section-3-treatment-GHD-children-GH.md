## 3.0: Overview And Primary Objectives

[Level II-2] A healthy child should experience normal growth and ultimately reach an adult height within his/her target height (TH) range. A GHD child is expected to be short, growing slowly, and to reach an extremely short adult height without treatment.

[Level I; Level II-2] Studies showed that normalisation of height during childhood and attainment of normal adult height could be achieved using the **lowest possible cumulative dose of GH**. [Level II-2; Level II-3] Cohort studies demonstrated that it is possible to achieve a final height (FH) within the TH range in idiopathic GHD patients when treated with GH from an early age.

**Primary objectives of GH therapy in GHD children:**
- Normalisation of height during childhood
- Attainment of normal adult height [Level III]

**Key Definitions:**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Target height range | ± 10 cm of the MPH |
| Final adult height achieved | Height gain <0.5 cm/year AND/OR bone age ≥15.5 years (girls) or ≥16.5 years (boys) |
| Near-adult height | Bone age ≥16 years (males) or ≥14 years (females) AND growth rate <2 cm/year for one year |

## 3.1: Initiation Of Therapy

[Level II-2; Level III] GH therapy should begin as soon as possible after diagnosis to optimise long-term growth. Every effort should be made to diagnose and treat children at the youngest possible age.

[Level II-2; Level II-3; Level II-2] Prospective cohort and controlled studies showed good catch-up growth when early rhGH replacement was initiated.

[Level II-3] In the National Cooperative Growth Study (NCGS), rhGH therapy started at peripubertal age resulted in insufficient increase in height SDS before the onset of puberty, leading to failure to achieve target height SDS. **Early initiation** and attainment of a **higher prepubertal height SDS** before the onset of puberty are important factors to improve final height in GHD children.

> **Recommendation:** GHD children should be treated with rhGH as soon as possible after the diagnosis is made. **(Grade C)**

## 3.2: Dosage

[Level II-3] 84% of GHD patients who received 0.025 mg/kg/day of rhGH for at least 5.4 years reached a final height within the normal population height range. [Level II-3] Data from the KIGS database (n=1,258) showed that those treated from an early age with a median rhGH dose of 0.025–0.028 mg/kg/day for at least 4 years achieved a FH within their TH range.

[Level I; Level II-2] Short-term and long-term studies showed that **higher doses** of rhGH resulted in significantly greater height velocity (HV), height for chronological age, and final height without any difference in bone age advancement, onset of puberty, fasting glucose, HbA1c, or lipid metabolism.

[Level I] A randomised controlled study demonstrated that height SDS at the end of treatment was significantly increased in those who received 0.05 or 0.1 mg/kg/day compared with those who received 0.025 mg/kg/day. However, another RCT found no significant difference between 0.05 and 0.1 mg/kg/day.

[Level I; Level II-1] Daily-treated patients had greater catch-up growth and total height gain compared with those who received 2, 3, or 4 times weekly injections at the same total weekly dose, with no significant difference in skeletal maturation or onset of puberty.

[Level I] Studies showed that growth response improved by titrating the GH dose to achieve higher IGF-1 targets or guided by individual GH responsiveness from a prediction model.

[Level II-3] Factors correlated with enhanced adult height: baseline height, younger age at onset of treatment, longer treatment duration (especially during pre-pubertal years), and greater growth velocity during the first year of treatment.

**Expected growth response at recommended GH dose:**
- Pre-treatment rate: 3–4 cm/year
- First year of therapy: **10–12 cm/year**
- Second and third year: **7–9 cm/year**
- Progressive waning of GH efficacy then occurs [Level I]

> **Recommendations:**
> - GH dose should be started at **0.025 mg/kg/day** and adjusted within the range of **0.025 to 0.05 mg/kg/day** based on growth response and IGF-1 level. **(Grade A)**
> - rhGH should be given as a **daily subcutaneous injection seven days a week**. **(Grade A)**
> - rhGH therapy should be given uninterrupted for **at least four years** prior to closure of the epiphyses. **(Grade C)**

## 3.3: Monitoring

[Level III] Routine follow-up for paediatric GHD patients should be performed by a paediatric endocrinologist in partnership with a paediatrician or primary care physician, conducted on a **3- to 6-monthly basis**, to document auxological data, monitor treatment response, compliance, and safety.

Adequate growth response is evidenced by:
- An increase in HV within the **first 6 months** of therapy
- An increase of at least **0.25 height SDS** over one year [Level III]

### IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 Monitoring

[Level III] To ensure adequate dosing, compliance, and safety (given the association between elevated serum IGF-1 and certain cancers), serum IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 should be monitored **at least yearly**.

[Level II-2] IGF-1 levels are highest at 6 weeks after starting GH and remain relatively constant thereafter. IGF-1 SDS correlates well with the dose of GH. [Level II-2] A larger increase in serum IGF-1 is associated with more impressive initial growth and greater first-year growth response.

### Dose Adjustments

For patients with **suboptimal growth response** (HV <2 cm/year above pre-treatment HV) over 6–12 months:
- First check patient compliance [Level III]

For **compliant patients** with poor response:
- If IGF-1 is low or normal → **increase rhGH dose** [Level III]
- If IGF-1 has exceeded the normal range → **stop rhGH** [Level III]
- If patient is already on maximum recommended dose → **stop rhGH** [Level III]
- If no increase in growth rate or serum IGF-1 over 6–12 months in a compliant patient → further treatment is generally futile

FDA approved dose ranges:
- Children: 0.025–0.050 mg/kg/day
- Adolescents: 0.025–0.10 mg/kg/day

### Bone Age Monitoring

[Level II-3] GH therapy guided by regular bone age (BA) monitoring has been shown to be useful. Subjects with BA assessment at entry, during the first year, and serially for the first 3 years had higher cumulative height SDS and height age change compared with those without serial BA assessments.

### Glucose and Thyroid Monitoring

[Level III] Patients with a history of childhood cancer or a diagnosis predisposing to malignancy should be monitored closely for recurrence or second malignancy.

[Level III] Monitoring of free T4 and TSH is of value for detecting hypothyroidism, which may develop during rhGH therapy.

[Level II-1; Level II-1] rhGH therapy has been shown to reduce insulin sensitivity with compensatory hyperinsulinaemia, increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in predisposed individuals.

[Level II-3] The incidence of T2DM was approximately **6-fold higher** in children treated with rhGH compared with age-matched untreated children. Diabetes did not resolve after rhGH was stopped, excluding a transient drug-induced effect. Each child's glucose status should be determined before starting rhGH therapy by measuring HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and insulin concentration.

High-risk groups for T2DM requiring closer monitoring:
- Obesity
- Turner syndrome
- Intrauterine growth retardation
- Prader-Willi syndrome
- GH deficiency secondary to other causes

> **Recommendation:** GHD children on rhGH therapy should be routinely monitored for:
> - Height: 3–6 monthly **(Grade C)**
> - Serum IGF-1: 3-monthly in the first year, then yearly **(Grade C)**
> - Free T4, TSH: yearly **(Grade C)**
> - Fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and insulin: before starting rhGH therapy **(Grade C)**
> - HbA1c: yearly or more frequently in patients at risk for T2DM **(Grade C)**
> - Bone age: prior to starting rhGH therapy and yearly thereafter **(Grade C)**
>
> In patients with poor GH response over 6–12 months (HV <2 cm/year above pre-treatment HV):
> - Check patient compliance **(Grade C)**
>
> In compliant patients with poor response:
> - If IGF-1 is low or normal → increase rhGH dose **(Grade C)**
> - If IGF-1 has exceeded the normal range → stop rhGH **(Grade C)**
> - If patient is already on maximum recommended dose → stop rhGH **(Grade C)**

## 3.4: Benefits Of rhGH Therapy In GHD Children

### a. Final Height

[Level II-3] Data from the KIGS database showed that differences between near-adult height and MPH ranged between -0.6 and -0.2 SDS after treatment. Results from Caucasian males with idiopathic GHD (IGHD) showed a median SDS of -2.4 at start of treatment and -0.8 at near-adult height after rhGH treatment.

**It is possible to attain target height in GHD children with optimal rhGH therapy.**

### b. Metabolic Effects

[Level II-2] GH therapy improved body composition by increasing lean tissue mass and reducing percentage body fat.

[Level II-1; Level II-2] rhGH therapy improved lipid profiles: reduction in total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides, and increase in HDL. The atherogenic index (total cholesterol/HDL) decreased with rhGH therapy.

[Level II-1] rhGH therapy showed a trend towards reduced insulin sensitivity with compensatory hyperinsulinaemia without untoward effects on glucose metabolism.

**Body composition improves and abnormal lipid profiles and atherogenic index improve with rhGH therapy in GHD children.**

### c. Bone Health

[Level II-2] Bone remodelling markers and bone mineral density (BMD) increase significantly during rhGH therapy.

[Level II-2] Replacement rhGH therapy has shown significant increase in areal BMD (aBMD) and volumetric BMD (vBMD). The mean values of lumbar aBMD and vBMD in treated GHD patients at final height did not differ from normative data. [Level II-1]

### d. Cardiovascular Effects

[Level II-1; Level II-3] GHD children have impaired left ventricular (LV) mass. The LV mass index was significantly lower in GHD children than controls and increased significantly after one year of GH replacement. [Level II-1] GHD children had normal global systolic function with subtle LV dysfunction. One year of GH treatment was associated with significant improvement of cardiac size and myocardial contractility.

**GHD children have impaired LV mass and subtle LV dysfunction; rhGH therapy normalises cardiac mass and function. However, the clinical significance of these findings remains unclear.**

### e. Quality of Life (QoL)

[Level III; Level II-1] No significant impairment in self-esteem, mood, and well-being was demonstrated in short children. There was no direct relationship between QoL and short stature resulting from Turner syndrome, chronic renal failure, GH deficiency, or idiopathic short stature.

[Level II-2] In a small cohort study, patients with acquired GH deficiency had QoL significantly below population norms, which improved with treatment, whereas those with idiopathic GH deficiency did not show significant changes.

[Level II-2] In a larger cohort study, behavioural scores significantly improved in patients with GH deficiency and ISS after rhGH therapy, with improvement noted in internalising subscales (withdrawn, somatic complications, anxiety/depression) and inattention, social problems, and thought problems.

**Behavioural scores partially improved in GHD children with rhGH therapy.**

## 3.5: Safety Of rhGH Therapy In Children

### a. Malignancy

rhGH is both anabolic and mitogenic; IGF-1 is antiapoptotic. Concerns exist that GH treatment may cause new malignancy (de novo), tumour recurrence, or second malignancy.

**New Malignancy:**
[Level II-3] Based on NCGS data, the incidence of leukaemia in rhGH-treated patients **without risk factors** was comparable to that in the general population. [Level II-3] Similarly in Japan, leukaemia incidence in rhGH-treated patients without risk factors was no greater than in the general population aged 0–15 years.

**Recurrent Malignancy:**
[Level II-3] Data from the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) showed the relative risk (RR) of disease recurrence was **0.83** for rhGH-treated survivors compared with those not treated with rhGH.

[Level II-3] GH outcomes from KIGS noted frequencies of recurrence: 11.7% for craniopharyngiomas, 4.7% for medulloblastomas, 8.8% for ependymomas, 4.0% for germinomas, and 9.8% for astrocytomas/gliomas.

[Level II-2] In craniopharyngioma, there was no evidence that rhGH replacement was associated with increased risk of tumour recurrence; 12.5% treated with rhGH developed tumour recurrence compared to 41.5% of those with no treatment. [Level II-3] No association between rhGH and progression-free survival was found in medulloblastoma.

**Second Malignancy:**
[Level II-3] The CCSS showed RR of a secondary neoplasm for rhGH-treated survivors was 3.21. This overall increase was driven by a small excess number of second neoplasms in acute leukaemia survivors (RR=4.98). The elevation of risk due to rhGH use appears to diminish with increasing length of follow-up. [Level II-3]

**Key Summary:**
- There is no evidence that leukaemia (de novo or relapse) or CNS tumour recurrence is increased with rhGH therapy.
- There is insufficient evidence regarding risk of secondary neoplasms with rhGH therapy.
- Patients with history of childhood cancer or diagnosis predisposing to malignancy should be monitored closely for recurrence or second malignancy.

### b. Benign Intracranial Hypertension (BIH)

[Level II-3] BIH has been recognised as an **early complication** of rhGH therapy. It has been reported in GH deficiency and also in Turner syndrome (TS), chronic renal insufficiency (CRI), Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS), glucocorticoids usage, and delayed puberty. Children receiving rhGH for CRI are more likely to develop BIH. [Level II-3]

[Level II-3] Based on an Australian and New Zealand national database, the incidence of BIH was **1.2 per 1,000 cases**. The KIGS database showed BIH occurred in 41 children (27.7 per 100,000 treatment-years). The median duration from onset of GH therapy to BIH ranged from 0.01 to 1.3 years.

**BIH is known to occur in patients on rhGH therapy.**

### c. Bone Health

[Level II-3] The NCGS database reported fewer than 1% of patients had scoliosis (general population prevalence 1.5–3.0%). [Level II-3] A retrospective study showed 4% of children treated with rhGH developed scoliosis. [Level II-2] Turner syndrome girls receiving rhGH therapy have a high prevalence of scoliosis (28%).

[Level II-3] Perthes' disease developed in 12 boys (69.9 per 100,000 patient-years) in the ICGS. Neither recurrence nor aggravation was observed during rhGH therapy.

[Level II-3; Level II-2] In the KIGS database, the incidence of slipped capital femoral epiphyses (SCFE) was 73.4 per 100,000 treatment-years. Incidence was significantly lower in IGHD (18.3) and ISS (14.5) than in TS (84.5), cranial tumours (86.1), and craniopharyngioma (120.5) groups.

**Children with organic causes of GH deficiency on rhGH therapy have an increased frequency of slipped capital femoral epiphyses.**

**There is insufficient evidence to suggest a causal relationship between rhGH therapy and scoliosis. However, the prevalence of scoliosis is high in TS girls.**

### d. Glucose Metabolism

[Level III] A study showed no significant change in glucose metabolism after 1 year of rhGH therapy (0.024 mg/kg/day) in children with idiopathic GH deficiency. [Level II-1] rhGH therapy in GHD children did not lead to impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) or T2DM, although it significantly decreased insulin sensitivity.

[Level II-3] The KIGS database demonstrated an increased frequency of T2DM in children receiving rhGH: 34 cases per 100,000 patient-years — approximately **6-fold higher** than untreated children.

**rhGH therapy decreases insulin sensitivity without impairing glucose tolerance. The risk of T2DM may be increased in individuals on rhGH therapy.**

### e. Skin

[Level II-3; Level II-2] There were no differences in naevi count between GHD children and control groups despite years of rhGH therapy. No neoplasia or pre-malignant naevi transformations were detected. Children with TS had more naevi, but there was no relation to duration of rhGH therapy and frequency of skin cancer.

**There was no increase in melanocytic naevi or risk of skin cancer in GHD patients on rhGH therapy. Melanocytic naevi were more common in TS but there was no increased frequency of skin cancer with rhGH therapy.**

### Other Rare Side Effects

[Level II-3] Other side effects are relatively rare and include: acute pancreatitis, gynaecomastia, oedema, lymphoedema, and carpal tunnel syndrome.
