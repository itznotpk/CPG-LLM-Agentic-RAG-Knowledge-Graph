## 5.1: Clinical Features Of Adult GH Deficiency Syndrome

Adults with GH deficiency present with a wide spectrum of clinical features — termed **GH deficiency syndrome**. However, the features are **not pathognomonic** and may overlap with a variety of other conditions. [Level III] Not all subjects with GH deficiency have the GHD syndrome.

[Level II-2] Untreated GH deficiency in adults is associated with **increased morbidity and mortality**. The syndrome is associated with:
- Increased cardiovascular mortality
- Osteopenia and fractures
- Increased fat mass, decreased muscle mass
- [Level III] Greater propensity for dyslipidaemia (increased total cholesterol, increased LDL, reduced HDL cholesterol)

[Level III] Successful rhGH replacement treatment ameliorates many symptoms of GH deficiency syndrome.

[Level II-2; Level II-2] GHD adults are associated with reduced bone density and osteoporosis. COGHD results in reduced bone density in adult life, and subjects with adult onset GHD have an increased prevalence of fractures.

[Level II-3] The KIMS database of GHD adults showed that the majority (60–70%) of patients had additional pituitary hormone deficiencies — primarily gonadotropin, TSH, and ACTH deficiency. Posterior pituitary deficiency was less common (22%).

> **Recommendation:** GHD adults should:
> - Be regularly monitored for cardiovascular parameters including lipid profiles, blood pressure, and fasting glucose **(Grade C)**
> - Undergo DEXA scan **(Grade C)**
> - Be screened for other pituitary hormone deficiencies and, if present, be optimally treated **(Grade A)**

## 5.2: Aetiology Of Adult GH Deficiency

A large number of different aetiologies contribute to GH deficiency in adulthood:

[Level II-3; Level II-3] The KIMS global surveillance database showed the most common causes: pituitary adenoma, idiopathic GH deficiency, and craniopharyngiomas (collectively 60–70% of all cases). Other causes:
- Non-pituitary brain tumours and irradiation: 7.0%
- Traumatic brain injury (TBI): 3.1%
- Postpartum pituitary necrosis: 2.9%
- Lymphocytic hypophysitis: 0.9%

[Level III] GH deficiency occurs in **one third** of patients who have undergone cranial irradiation for non-pituitary brain tumours.

[Level II-2] GH deficiency occurs in **two thirds** of patients with childhood onset acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL).

[Level I] In a systematic review, up to one third of TBI patients were diagnosed to have GH deficiency. In the majority, the condition was **transient** — only **16%** had persistent deficiency at 6–12 months of follow-up. [Level III] Screening for GH deficiency is appropriate **6–12 months** after moderate-to-severe head trauma.

> **Recommendations:**
> - GH deficiency should be excluded in adult patients with **clinical features of GH deficiency AND one of the following:** hypothalamic-pituitary disease, previous cranial irradiation, or traumatic brain injury. **(Grade B)**
> - In the **absence** of hypothalamic-pituitary disease, screening for GH deficiency is not recommended in adults. **(Grade C)**

## 5.3: Diagnosis Of Adult GH Deficiency

The diagnosis should be based on **correct identification of clinical features** in individuals at risk, combined with **biochemical confirmation** via stimulation testing.

[Level III] Commonly used tests: ITT, glucagon stimulation test (GST), and arginine with/without GHRH (ARG+GHRH). **GHRH is not available in Malaysia.** The ITT and ARG+GHRH tests were better at differentiating GHD from normal. In the KIMS global database, ITT and ARG+GHRH were the most commonly used. [Level II-3]

### Diagnostic Cut-Off

[Level III] The cut-off value to diagnose GH deficiency in adults is **GH <5.1 mcg/L** (immunochemiluminometric assay). This provides:
- Sensitivity: 96%
- Specificity: 92%

[Level II-3] If a patient has **3 or 4 pituitary hormone deficiencies** in addition to a low IGF-1 level (below -2 SD), in the absence of conditions that lower IGF-1, the probability of GH deficiency being documented on stimulation testing is **>90%**, and thus stimulation test may not be necessary.

[Level III] In most circumstances, only **one** stimulation test is required for biochemical diagnosis in adults.

### ITT — Gold Standard for Adults

**Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT) is the gold standard for diagnosing adult GH deficiency.**

ITT is **contraindicated** in: [Level III]
- History of ischaemic heart disease or electrocardiographic evidence thereof
- History of seizure disorders [Level III]
- History of previous stroke (prudent to avoid)
- Age >55 years

When ITT is contraindicated, the **Glucagon Stimulation Test (GST)** is a reliable alternative because of: [Level III]
1. Accurate and reliable discrimination between normal and true GH deficiency
2. Availability
3. Reproducibility
4. Safety
5. Lack of influence by gender or hypothalamic cause of GHD

> **Recommendations:**
> - ITT should be avoided in patients with: history of seizures, history of strokes, presence of coronary artery disease, or age >55 years. **(Grade C)**
> - Glucagon stimulation test is an alternative in patients unsuitable for ITT. **(Grade C)**
> - Patients with irreversible hypothalamic-pituitary lesions AND evidence of at least 3 pituitary hormone deficiencies AND serum IGF-1 levels below age- and sex-appropriate reference range are deemed GH deficient and do not require GH stimulation test. **(Grade C)**
> - rhGH should only be prescribed to patients with **clinical features suggestive of adult GH deficiency AND biochemically proven GH deficiency**. **(Grade C)**

## 5.4: Benefits Of rhGH Therapy In GHD Adults

### 5.4.1: Psychological Well-Being

[Level I; Level II-3; Level III] The majority of studies showed that adults with hypopituitarism of both childhood and adult onset have a **diminished QoL** compared to the normal population. Reductions in physical and mental energy, dissatisfaction with body image, and poor memory have been reported most consistently.

[Level I; Level III; Level II-3; Level II-1; Level II-2; Level III] GH replacement in GHD adults has **improved QoL**, especially in the domains of energy level, self-esteem, attention, and memory, with considerable social impact including fewer days of sick leave.

[Level III] Quantitative indices of QoL are increased within **1–3 months** in most patients, although some require longer treatment (6 months) before improvements are noted. If the QoL of the patient is normal at baseline, no improvement will be seen with rhGH therapy.

**Methods for assessing QoL in GHD adults:**
- Generic Questionnaires: Nottingham Health Profile, Psychological General Health Well-being Schedule, General Health Questionnaire
- Disease-Specific Questionnaires: Quality of Life-Adult with GH Deficiency Assessment, GH Deficiency Questionnaire

> **Recommendations:**
> - A QoL questionnaire should be administered **before beginning GH replacement** as psychological well-being is the most compelling indication for GH replacement in adults with GH deficiency. **(Grade C)**
> - Adults with GH deficiency on GH replacement should be evaluated using the QoL questionnaire at **6- to 12-monthly** intervals. **(Grade C)**

### 5.4.2: Body Composition

[Level III; Level II-2; Level II-1] There is a **reduction of total body fat mass (FM)** after rhGH replacement, with the magnitude dose-dependent. This FM reduction is accompanied by a parallel increase in fat-free mass (FFM).

[Level III; Level II; Level II-2; Level II-2; Level II-1] The reduction in whole body FM with rhGH replacement is mainly due to a marked **decrease in central and visceral fat** in GHD subjects. [Level III; Level II-2; Level II-1] rhGH replacement has been shown to have greater reduction in central adipose tissue compared to peripheral adipose tissue.

[Level III] Body composition changes were more pronounced for young and lean subjects. [Level III] While the increase in FFM was seen within a month of rhGH replacement, the increase in **muscle strength** was only seen after **6–12 months** of rhGH replacement. This lag may be due to initial improvement in muscle tissue hydration.

> **Recommendation:** GHD adults undergoing rhGH replacement should have their body composition (BMI and waist circumference) measured regularly. **(Grade C)**

### 5.4.3: Cardiovascular Risk

[Level I; Level III] Patients with GH deficiency have increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. There is an associated increase in cardiovascular risk markers similar to Insulin Resistance Syndrome (IRS): central obesity, elevated triglyceride, and reduced HDL cholesterol.

[Level II-2; Level II-2] Higher incidence of diabetes and hypertension has been reported in GHD adults. [Level II-3] Doppler ultrasound has shown increased carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) in GHD adults, indicating the presence of atherosclerosis.

[Level I; Level II-3; Level III] Replacement with rhGH in GHD adults has shown a **reduction in fat mass** (particularly centrally distributed) and **improvement in lipid levels** (reduction in total cholesterol and LDL).

[Level III] The effects of GH replacement on insulin resistance are highly dependent on the dose used and patient susceptibility to diabetes. Susceptible patients (obese, family history of diabetes, previous gestational diabetes) should be given a **very low starting dose of 0.1–0.2 mg/day**, with the dose slowly increased based on clinical response. There may be an **initial deterioration of insulin sensitivity** with rhGH replacement in the first 3 months, but insulin sensitivity returns to baseline with longer duration of replacement.

[Level II-2; Level II-3] Longer term studies (5-year duration) demonstrated a significant reduction in IMT, suggesting a **reversal of the atherosclerotic process**. However, no data yet shows reduction in cardiovascular events or mortality.

> **Recommendation:** GHD adults undergoing GH replacement should have their cardiovascular parameters monitored in addition to body composition measurements. **(Grade C)**

### 5.4.4: Bone Density

[Level II-1] COGHD results in reduced bone density in adult life; subjects with adult onset GHD have an increased prevalence of fractures.

[Level III; Level II-2] Treating GHD adults with rhGH replacement improved BMD as assessed by DEXA scan, more pronounced in males and in those with lower initial BMD.

> **Recommendations:**
> - GHD adults should undergo DEXA scan **prior to starting GH replacement**. **(Grade B)**
> - If initial bone density is abnormal, repeat DEXA scan is recommended at **1–3 year intervals** to assess the need for additional anti-osteoporosis treatment. **(Grade C)**

## 5.5: Dosage In GHD Adults

[Level I; Level I; Level II-1] The rhGH dosage should be **individualised**, independent of body weight, and increased slowly to the **minimal dose that normalises serum IGF-1 level** without untoward side effects. In older patients, lower starting doses should be used.

**Dosing Schedule:**
- Starting dose: **0.1–0.2 mg daily for men** and **0.2–0.3 mg daily for women**
- At each visit, increase by **0.1–0.2 mg** until maintenance dose is reached based on clinical response, IGF-1 levels, and side effects [Level III]

> **Recommendations:**
> - rhGH dose for replacement in GHD adults should be **individualised independent of body weight**. **(Grade A)**
> - Starting dose: **0.1 mg daily for men** and **0.2 mg daily for women**. The dose is increased by 0.1–0.2 mg to achieve a maintenance dose based on clinical response, IGF-1 levels, and side effects. **(Grade C)**

## 5.6: Safety Of rhGH In GHD Adults

### 5.6.1: Common Adverse Effects

[Level I; Level I] The most common adverse events in short-term RCTs were **oedema, arthralgia, and muscle weakness**. [Level I] A 3-year RCT reported fluid retention adverse events: arthralgia (30.6%), myalgia (13.5%), peripheral oedema (12.6%), paresthesia (9.0%), carpal tunnel syndrome (7.2%), generalised oedema (7.2%), and hypoaesthesia (5.4%). [Level II-3] The KIMS database reported 18% of patients developed fluid retention.

**Fluid-related adverse effects such as arthralgia, myalgia, and peripheral oedema are common in rhGH replacement therapy.**

### 5.6.2: Risk of Tumour Recurrence

[Level II-2; Level II-2; Level II-2] Three observational studies showed that rhGH replacement therapy had **no significant effect on tumour regrowth**. [Level II-2] rhGH treatment was not a significant independent predictor of recurrence in non-functioning pituitary adenoma treated by surgical removal.

**rhGH in GHD adults does not increase recurrence of hypothalamic-pituitary tumours.**

### 5.6.3: Risk of New Malignant Disease

[Level II-2] A prospective longitudinal study of rhGH therapy in hypopituitary males over 50 years old demonstrated **no change in serum PSA**. [Level II-3] The KIMS database showed no evidence of rhGH therapy increasing the risk of developing malignant neoplasms.

[Level II-2] When comparing hypopituitary adults without rhGH replacement versus those on long-term GH replacement with the normal population, malignancies were increased in those **without** rhGH replacement. In patients **on** rhGH replacement, overall mortality and rate of malignancies were **similar to the normal population**.

**rhGH replacement in GHD adults does not increase the risk of new malignant tumours.**
