## 3.1: Turner Syndrome (TS)

Turner Syndrome (TS) is a genetic disorder due to abnormalities of the X chromosome resulting in an array of genetic, developmental, endocrine, cardiovascular, psychosocial, and reproductive issues. [Level II-2] It affects approximately **1 in every 2,500 liveborn females**. Majority have extreme short stature, gonadal dysgenesis, and hypogonadism with normal intellectual performance and language abilities; however, visual-spatial and/or visual-perceptual abilities are impaired.

[Level II-1] The average height of untreated women with TS is **143 cm** — approximately **20 cm below the target height**. [Level II-3] They are able to reach normal adult height with high doses of rhGH.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

**a. Final Height:**
[Level I; Level II-1] A Cochrane Systematic Review showed that rhGH was effective in improving growth and final height in girls with TS. The final height was 148 cm (1.4 ± 1.0 SD Turner chart) in treated women versus 141 cm (0.2 ± 0.9 SD Turner chart) in untreated women.

[Level III] Factors affecting height gain (from start of rhGH to near-adult height):
- Mid-parental height
- Height at start of rhGH
- Index of responsiveness during first year on rhGH
- Mean dose of rhGH per week
- Age at start of puberty
- Age at start of rhGH
- Duration of rhGH therapy
- Birth weight

**b. Cognitive Function and Psychological Adjustment:**
[Level I] The Cochrane Review showed the possibility that girls treated with rhGH have better psychological adjustment than untreated girls. [Level III] However, there were no demonstrable effects on cognitive function, visual-spatial or visual-perceptual abilities.

**c. Bone/Body Composition:**
[Level II-2] rhGH therapy had little effect on cortical or trabecular BMD in girls with TS. However, rhGH was associated with increased lean body mass and reduced adiposity.

### When to Start rhGH

[Level III] rhGH therapy should be considered **as soon as falling height percentile is demonstrated** in the linear growth of the child with TS, and the decision to start is best directed by a paediatric endocrinologist.

### Dose

[Level I] rhGH doses range from **0.045 to 0.055 mg/kg/day**. Administered as a subcutaneous injection 6 or 7 days per week.

### Oestrogen Therapy

[Level I] Oestrogen replacement should **not be started before the age of 12 years** in rhGH-treated TS patients. [Level II-2] TS patients in whom oestrogen was delayed until 15 years of age gained an average of 8.4 ± 4.3 cm over projected final height, versus only 5.1 ± 3.6 cm for those who started at 12 years.

However, timing of oestrogen replacement should also consider age-appropriate pubertal maturation, bone health, and psychosocial stress of delayed sexual maturation. [Level II-3; Level III] The dose of oestrogen should be increased gradually over 2–3 years:
- Starting dose: Conjugated oestrogen (premarin) 0.3 mg every other day OR 5 mcg ethinyl oestradiol daily
- Increase gradually every 12 months to: premarin 0.625 mg daily OR ethinyl oestradiol 20 mcg daily
- Then add medroxyprogesterone acetate (provera) 5 mg daily for 12 days to initiate cyclical uterine bleeding

### Duration of Treatment

[Level III] rhGH therapy may be continued until **satisfactory height is attained** or **bone age is 14 years** or near FH is attained.

### Monitoring

[Level III] Patients should be monitored as any other patient on GH therapy. IGF-1 should be monitored to ensure value within ± 2 SD.

### Adverse Reactions

[Level I] Girls with TS on rhGH therapy are at increased risk for: diabetes mellitus, SCFE, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, oedema, lymphoedema, or scoliosis. [Level II-2] Seven deaths were reported in 5,220 patients (5 from aortic dissection/rupture) and 10 new-onset malignancies. [Level III] rhGH therapy does not appear to affect the diameter of ascending or descending aorta.

> **Recommendations:**
> - Turner syndrome with short stature should be considered for rhGH therapy and referred early to a paediatric endocrinologist. **(Grade A)**
> - Oestrogen replacement therapy in TS children should not be initiated before the age of 12 years. **(Grade A)**

## 3.2: Small For Gestational Age (SGA)

[Level III] SGA is defined as birth weight and/or birth length of **≥2 SD below the mean for gestational age**. Approximately 2.3–10% of all infants are born SGA. Most achieve appropriate catch-up growth by 2 years of age. However, approximately **15% do not catch up** and continue to experience poor growth throughout childhood. [Level III] Catch-up growth is considered achieved when the child's height is at or above -2 SD for age.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

**a. Final Height:**
[Level II-1] SGA subjects were significantly shorter than normal birth weight peers (men 4.5 cm shorter, women 3.9 cm shorter) at age 20. [Level III] rhGH therapy (0.033 or 0.067 mg/kg/day) commenced at 8 years of age and continued for 7–8 years increased adult height by ≥1 SD. [Level II-1] No significant difference in height gain between low dose (0.033 mg/kg/day) and high dose (0.067 mg/kg/day).

**b. Cognitive Function:** [Level III] There is inconclusive evidence that rhGH improves IQ in short SGA children.

**c. Body Composition:** [Level III; Level II-1] rhGH therapy improved body composition, blood pressure, and lipid metabolism in short SGA children.

**d. Bone Mineral Density:** [Level II-1] BMAD (-0.6 SDS) was significantly reduced at start but normalised (0.3 SDS) during rhGH therapy.

**e. Cardiovascular Risk:** [Level II-1] Children born SGA treated with rhGH who had elevated systolic blood pressure before treatment showed a reduction in blood pressure over time. [Level II-1] Total cholesterol and LDL decreased significantly with rhGH therapy.

### When to Start

[Level II-3] With reference to normal population standards: 44% of infants caught up at 3 months of age, 51% at 3 years, 66% at 4 years, and 73% at 6 years. [Level III] In most published studies, rhGH therapy was initiated after 5 years of age.

[Level III] EMEA approved rhGH use in short SGA children (birth weight and/or birth length below -2 SD) with:
- Height SDS <-2.5 AND parental-adjusted height SD <-1.0
- Absence of catch-up growth (HV <0 SD during last 1 year)
- Age of start of treatment >4 years
- Recommended GH dose: **0.035 mg/kg/day**

### Safety

[Level III; Level II-1] Adverse events are not more common in SGA than in other conditions treated with rhGH.

**a. Insulin Resistance:** [Level III; Level II-1] Children born SGA tend to develop higher fasting insulin level and relative insulin resistance during rhGH therapy; these changes appear largely reversible when treatment is terminated.

**b. Bone Age Acceleration:** [Level II-1] rhGH is associated with acceleration of bone maturation, especially in the first 2 years, regardless of dose.

**c. Risk of Malignancy:** [Level III; Level II-1] There is no evidence that the risk of malignancy is increased with rhGH therapy.

**d. BIH:** BIH is a rare complication, occurring in approximately **1 in 1,000 individuals**. [Level III; Level II-1]

> **Recommendation:** SGA children who remained short (<3rd percentile) after 4 years of age should be referred to a paediatric endocrinologist for evaluation and consideration of rhGH therapy. **(Grade C)**

## 3.3: Idiopathic Short Stature (ISS)

[Level I] ISS describes children who are very short for their age for unknown reason. A systematic review showed that 5–9% of the shortest 3% of the population did not reach an adult height above -2 SD, accounting for 0.2% of the normal children population.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

**a. Final Height:** [Level I] ISS patients treated with rhGH for 6 months to 6.2 years showed near-final heights in girls 7.5 cm taller than untreated controls. Another study reported adult height 3.7 cm taller in the GH-treated group. Short-term height gain ranges from none to approximately 0.7 SD over one year. [Level I] The estimated potential cost of rhGH therapy is around USD 52,000 per 2.54 cm gain in height.

**b. QoL:** [Level I] Significant improvement in QoL in rhGH-treated ISS children compared with controls; no significant impact on psychological adaptation or self-perception.

**c. Psychosocial Functioning:** [Level II-3] rhGH therapy did not improve psychosocial functioning. [Level II-2] Psychological and social functioning in ISS children was normal compared to Dutch population norms.

### Dose

rhGH doses range from 0.30 to 0.37 mg/kg/week (0.043–0.053 mg/kg/day). [Level I] The dose of 0.053 mg/kg/day was effective to achieve normal adult height in 94% of patients. The mean overall height gain was 7.2 cm at 0.053 mg/kg/day versus 5.4 cm at 0.034 mg/kg/day. [Level III; Level II-2] Higher dose did not appear to accelerate pubertal onset, pace, or bone maturation.

> **Recommendation:** rhGH should not be routinely prescribed in ISS patients for the following reasons:
> - Inconsistent height gain with rhGH therapy
> - High dose requirement (cost issues)
> - Absence of psychological and social malfunctioning in ISS without therapy **(Grade C)**

## 3.4: Familial/Genetic Short Stature (FSS)

FSS is characterised by:
- Height below the 3rd percentile for age
- Normal growth velocity
- Family history of short stature in one or both parents
- Normal physical examination (including body proportion)
- Normal bone age (appropriate for chronological age)
- Normal puberty
- Expected adult height below 3rd percentile but within target height range (MPH ± 10 cm)

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

[Level II-3] A small RCT using two different doses (0.031 or 0.063 mg/kg/day) of rhGH in FSS showed **no difference in adult height outcome**. The incremental effect of both doses on stature was minimal.

> **Recommendation:** rhGH should not be used in familial/genetic short stature. **(Grade C)**

## 3.5: Chronic Renal Insufficiency (CRI)

Growth retardation in CRI is influenced by nutritional insufficiency, endocrine and metabolic alterations, and glucocorticoid/immunosuppressive therapy. Renal failure is a **state of GH resistance**, not GH deficiency.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

[Level I] The FH of CRI patients treated with rhGH improved by 0.5–1.7 SDS across 15 RCTs, whereas the control group lost by 0.5 SDS. When higher dose (0.05 mg/kg/day) was used, height velocity was 3.8 cm/year above the untreated group. The growth response is negatively correlated with age, height at start of rhGH, and duration of dialysis. [Level III] The highest treatment success is achieved when treatment is started early with relatively well-preserved residual renal function.

[Level III] AACE Guideline 2003 has **not recommended** rhGH therapy for post-transplantation patients unless it is given as part of a research study.

### Adverse Reactions

[Level I] Side effects in CRI patients were similar to control groups: wheezing, acute rejection in transplanted patients, deterioration in kidney function, raised fasting glucose, papilloedema, glucose intolerance, granuloma formation, lymph node swelling, claudication, hypertension, and worsening of pre-existing idiopathic scoliosis.

> **Recommendation:** In local setting, rhGH therapy should not be given to short children with CRI. **(Grade C)**

## 3.6: Prader-Willi Syndrome (PWS)

[Level III] PWS occurs in 1 in 10,000–16,000 live-born infants. It is caused by paternal deletion or maternal disomy of genes on the long arm of chromosome 15. [Level II-1; Level III] Clinical features: short stature, mental retardation, excessive body fat, decreased muscle mass, sleep disturbances, and central apnoea.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

[Level II-1] Body composition improved but did not normalise. Lean body mass (LBM) improved at near-adult height but remained low. Body fat percentage and fat/lean ratio improved during the first year of rhGH therapy but returned to pretreatment state at near-adult height. All patients reached near-adult height within mid-parental height range.

### Adverse Reactions

**a. Death:** [Level III] In a mini review of 13 mortality reports, 10 out of 13 PWS children died of respiratory insufficiency. The highest risk of mortality occurred during the **first 7 months** of rhGH therapy.

**b. Respiratory:** [Level II-2] PWS children had increased risk of central apnoea. However, 6 months of rhGH therapy did not aggravate sleep-related breathing disorders in young PWS children.

**c. Carbohydrate Metabolism:** [Level II-2; Level II-3] All PWS children receiving rhGH had normal levels of insulin, glucose, and HOMA-IR during the study. [Level II-1] However, in the KIGS database (n=22), one patient developed IGT and another T2DM when weight increased rapidly (>10 kg in 6 months).

**d. Scoliosis:** [Level II-3] The prevalence of scoliosis in PWS is high (37.5%) and increases with age. [Level III; Level II-2] However, the prevalence of scoliosis is **not higher** in PWS patients receiving rhGH.

**Local Context:** PWS patients in Malaysia are generally very obese with high risk of central apnoea at presentation. rhGH dose based on body weight is likely to be very high and hence very expensive.

> **Recommendation:** rhGH therapy should not be given to children with PWS. **(Grade C)**

## 3.7: Noonan Syndrome (NS)

[Level II-3; Level II-2] NS is a multiple congenital abnormality syndrome characterised by short stature, mental retardation, distinctive facial appearance, congenital heart defects, thoracic deformities, and cryptorchidism. The incidence is between 1:1,000 and 1:2,500 live births. It is a hereditary autosomal dominant disorder, but 60% of cases are sporadic. Mutation of PTPN11 gene accounts for approximately 50% of all cases.

### Outcome of rhGH Therapy

**a. Final Height:**
FH gain was variable with rhGH therapy. [Level III] Some rhGH-treated NS patients had no or only small increases in final adult height. The poor FH may be partly explained by PTPN11 gene mutation.

[Level II-3] In the KIGS Database (n=402) treated for 4–12 years, the median increment in final height was **0.6 SDS**. [Level II-3] In an uncontrolled study of 29 patients using 0.05 mg/kg/day over 3–10.3 years, mean height gain was 1.3 SDS (range +0.2 to +2.7).

### Adverse Reactions

[Level II-3; Level II-2] NS patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy may be at risk from rhGH therapy due to GH effects on cardiac muscle mass. [Level II-2] Possible abnormal anabolic effects of rhGH on myocardial thickness are not confirmed, and none of the rhGH-treated NS patients developed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

**Local Context:** rhGH cannot be recommended for short children with Noonan syndrome in view of the high dose and cost, wide variation of growth response, and uncertainty of rhGH on cardiovascular safety.

> **Recommendation:** rhGH therapy should not be given to children with Noonan syndrome. **(Grade C)**

## 3.8: Russell-Silver Syndrome (RSS)

RSS has an incidence of 1:100,000 live births. Key features include intrauterine and postnatal growth retardation, triangular facies, and limb asymmetry. There is a tendency for fasting hypoglycaemia in early childhood and genital dysmorphism (cryptorchidism, hypospadias, hypogonadism).

### Growth Response with GH Therapy

[Level III] Recent data suggested RSS exhibits abnormal pulsatility of GH secretion. [Level III] In an earlier review it was suggested that FH outcome can be improved by using pharmacological doses of rhGH. However, the number of RSS patients studied was very small. [Level III] Height gain (+1.3 SD) was the same in SGA and RSS, but final adult height is lower in RSS as they were shorter at baseline.

> **Recommendation:** rhGH should not be given to children with Russell-Silver syndrome. **(Grade C)**

## 3.9: Skeletal Dysplasia

[Level III; Level II-3] The incidence of skeletal dysplasia is at least 30–45 per 100,000 newborns. Achondroplasia (ACH) and hypochondroplasia (HCH) are the most common. ACH has an estimated incidence of about 4–5 per 100,000.

ACH is characterised by short-limb dwarfism, macrocephaly with prominent forehead, midfacial hypoplasia, lumbar lordosis, trident configuration of hands, and hydrocephalus.

[Level III] The FH in untreated ACH: 131.5 cm (boys) and 125 cm (girls). [Level II-3] HCH has FH of 145.0–165.0 cm (untreated boys) and 133.4–150.6 cm (untreated girls).

### Goals and Outcomes of GH Treatment

First-year response to rhGH therapy is typically a **2–3 cm increase in growth velocity** in prepubertal children with skeletal dysplasia, or a height gain of **0.5 SDS or less** from a baseline level of -4 to -5 SDS.

[Level III] rhGH therapy for up to 5 years in ACH can produce a total height gain of about 1 SDS. However, FH data is lacking. Body proportion (trunk to leg length ratio) does not seem to be affected by rhGH therapy.

[Level III] **rhGH therapy is unlikely to improve ACH height to within the normal range**. On the contrary, rhGH therapy can **worsen scoliosis** during pubertal growth. Surgical limb lengthening is a better alternative for ACH patients.

[Level II-3] Height improvement was much greater in HCH than in ACH. With 3 years of rhGH therapy, mean increase in height SD was negligible for ACH (-0.2 to 0.1 SD) but better for HCH (1.2 to 2.6 SD).

> **Recommendation:** rhGH therapy should not be used for skeletal dysplasia. **(Grade C)**
