> **Context:** This section covers safe medication use during general anaesthesia, including volatile anaesthetic agents, medical gas safety, cylinder handling, piped gas systems, fluid administration, TIVA/TCI monitoring, neuromuscular blocking agents, and GLP-1 receptor agonist preoperative management.

---

## Key Points for General Anaesthesia

1. **Volatile anaesthetic agents and medical gas cylinders should be stored safely.**

2. **Opened volatile anaesthetic bottles need to be labelled** with the date they were opened, and empty bottles should be properly disposed of to avoid reuse.

3. **Regular maintenance and checks of anaesthetic machines, vaporisers, breathing circuits and gas cylinders are essential.**

4. **Before starting a case, ensure stable and constant supply of pipeline gases** by checking pressure gauges.

5. **Smart Infusion pump technology use in perioperative settings is recommended** to ensure precise fluid delivery.

6. **Fluid warming devices are recommended** to prevent inadvertent hypothermia during perioperative fluid management.

7. **Monitoring of depth of anaesthesia via processed EEG is indicated when using TIVA.**

8. **Neuromuscular monitoring should be available** whenever a neuromuscular blocking agent is administered.

9. **If patient is taking medications that delay gastric emptying**, withhold them before scheduled procedures when possible. If not feasible, consider delaying surgery or treat patient as having a 'full stomach'.

## 3.1.1 Handling of Inhalational Agents / Volatile Agents

a. Given the high potency and potential lethality of volatile anaesthetic agents, particularly in liquid form, it is crucial to **securely store bottles, including those already opened**.

b. **Refilling of vaporisers using unique key-fill systems must be strictly practised.** Check vaporiser is adequately filled but not overfilled and filling ports are tightly closed.

c. **Partially emptied bottles need to be labelled with the date they were opened.**

d. **Empty bottles should have caps removed** and be disposed of in suitable container to prevent inadvertent reuse or refilling with different liquid.

e. **Regular maintenance and checks** of anaesthetic machines, vaporisers and breathing circuits to prevent leakage and environmental pollution. Vaporiser leak test per manufacturer recommendations before each use.

f. **Large spills require specific cleaning and containment procedures.** Institutions need protocols on spill management which must be complied with.

## 3.1.2 Medical Gases

### a. Storage of Medical Gas Cylinders

**Health and safety risks to mitigate:**
- Cylinders are heavy and can cause severe injuries if mishandled
- Contain compressed gas at high pressure - severe injury or death if damage leads to sudden gas escape
- Oxygen supports combustion and increases fire risk

**Storage Requirements:**
1. Store in cool, dry, well-ventilated area, away from heat and ignition sources
2. Store in secure and upright position
3. Post warning notices prohibiting smoking and naked lights (clearly visible)
4. Regular inspection for corrosion, leaks, valve/cylinder damage
5. Adequate space for segregation of different gases and full/empty cylinders
6. Daily checks with documentation for signs of leakage
7. Use full cylinders in strict rotation according to expiry dates
8. Maintain records for each type at each location for inventory control

### b. Handling Medical Gas Cylinders

1. All personnel must receive **regular manual handling training**
2. Trained personnel should be aware of hazards and emergency procedures
3. Wear appropriate **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**
4. Handle with care - never knock violently or allow to fall over
5. **Never lift cylinders by the neck**
6. Move only with appropriate trolley; close valve when moving with apparatus attached
7. Secure to suitable support when in use
8. **Never roll cylinders along ground** (may cause valve to open accidentally)
9. **Turn off/fully close when not in use**

### c. Use of Cylinders

1. **No lubrication or oil/grease contamination** of valve or equipment
2. Care with hand creams - could cause ignition when valve turned on
3. Before use, confirm:
   - Gas type via cylinder identification label (primary means of identification)
   - Cylinder product and filling pressure compatible with regulator
   - Cylinder not expired; leave spindle key in valve for emergency closure
4. Ensure equipment operating instructions available
5. Regular checks while in use for sufficient content and no leaks

### d. Handling of Oxygen Cylinders and Regulators

1. **Full training** in cylinder use, regulator attachment, and fire risks
2. **Clean hands before handling** - combustion risk from oils and grease
3. Adequately dry hands after alcohol gels
4. Clean clothing free from oil and combustible contaminants
5. Ensure outlet and inlet are clean before attaching regulator
6. **Open cylinder slowly and check for leaks**
7. Ensure adequate ventilation when using medical oxygen

### e. Piped Medical Gases

1. **Check pressure gauges** (400-500 kPa) before starting a case
2. Anaesthesia providers responsible for gases from terminal outlet to patient
3. Identify gases being supplied by pipeline, confirm with **'tug test'**
4. All professionals should know safety practices: colour coded Shrader sockets/probes, colour coded hoses, non-interchangeable connections
5. Check apparatus connected to oxygen supply with adequate reserve cylinder
6. All cylinders on GA machines securely seated and turned off after checking contents

## 3.1.3 Fluid Administration

a. **Smart Infusion pump technology is recommended** for administration of IV solutions in perioperative/procedural environments to minimise complications from excessive and insufficient fluid delivery, particularly in:
   - Paediatric patients
   - Geriatric patients

b. **Gravity infusions** may be used for IV solutions if solely utilised as flush solution (carrier fluid) or for immediate fluid resuscitation needs.

c. **Rapid IV infusion systems** should be available for rapid administration of fluids and blood when required. Anaesthesia healthcare professionals should have comprehensive knowledge and skills to prevent potential harm from its use.

d. **Fluid warming devices are recommended** for perioperative fluid management to prevent inadvertent hypothermia.

---

## 3.1.4 Target-Controlled Infusion / Total Intravenous Anaesthesia Monitoring

a. When administering drugs via infusion:
   - **Label the patient end of the infusion line**
   - Use one-way valves to prevent unintended syphoning
   - Use dedicated IV cannula when feasible
   - Take precaution to avoid bolus injection of residual drugs in tubing at end of TIVA/TCI

b. **Use TCI when maintaining general anaesthesia with TIVA.** Before determining initial bolus and subsequent infusion rates:
   - Choose drug and pharmacokinetic model
   - Input patient characteristics (age, gender, body weight)

c. **Monitoring of depth of anaesthesia via processed EEG is indicated when TIVA is practised.**

d. **Standardise concentration of propofol and dilution of remifentanil** in anaesthesia departments to reduce medication error.

e. **Place syringes containing drugs in pump prior to programming** infusion pumps.

f. IV cannula used for infusion should remain **visible throughout anaesthesia** whenever feasible.

g. **Same standards of practice and monitoring apply** when administering TIVA outside the operating room.

**Neuromuscular monitoring should be available whenever a neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA) is used.**

## 3.1.6 Drugs Affecting Gastric Emptying

### Background

Several drugs used in perioperative period can result in:
- Delayed gastric emptying
- Increased risk for regurgitation and aspiration

**Examples:**
- Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists
- Opioids
- Anti-Parkinson's medication (levodopa)

When possible, **withhold these medications prior to scheduled procedures.** When not feasible, consider delaying surgery or treating patient as 'full stomach'.

### Special Considerations for GLP-1 Agonists

**Background:**
- Oral hypoglycaemic agent also used for weight reduction
- Causes delayed gastric emptying as mechanism of action
- Examples: Liraglutide

### GLP-1 Agonist Preoperative Management

**Irrespective of indication, dose, or procedure type:**

| Dosing Schedule | Recommendation |
|-----------------|----------------|
| **Daily dosing** | Withhold on day of procedure/surgery or three half-lives |
| **Weekly dosing** | Withhold one week prior to procedure/surgery |

**On day of surgery:**
- Assess for GI symptoms of GLP-1 effects indicating delayed gastric emptying:
  - Vomiting
  - Retching
  - Bloating

### Preoperative Gastric Ultrasound

**Recommended when:**
- GLP-1 agonists not adequately withheld
- Patient has symptoms of delayed gastric emptying

**Based on gastric ultrasound findings:**
- If stomach is full, inconclusive, or not possible to perform → **consider delaying procedure**
- When delaying surgery is not feasible → **treat as 'full stomach'** and manage accordingly

### Additional Considerations

- **No current evidence** supports optimal preoperative fasting duration for GLP-1 patients
- General preoperative fasting guidelines may not apply to patients taking GLP-1 agonists
- If GLP-1 agonists (prescribed for diabetes) held longer than dosing schedule → **consult endocrinologist** for bridging antidiabetic therapy to avoid hyperglycaemia
