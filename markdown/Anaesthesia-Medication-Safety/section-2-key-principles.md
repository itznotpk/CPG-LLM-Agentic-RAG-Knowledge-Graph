> **Context:** This section covers all key principles for safe medication use in anaesthesia — medication management workflow, purchasing, storage, reconciliation, the 5Rs concept, anaesthesia cart organisation, drug preparation, syringe labelling (including TML and colour-coded standards), safe administration of high-concentration drugs (refer Table 2: Medications via Central Venous Catheter), IV delivery protocols, extravasation injury prevention/management, intra-arterial injection management, infection control, and documentation requirements.

---

## Key Points for Section 2

1. **Optimising medication management workflow and processes ensures safe medication use** which includes efficient purchasing, appropriate storage, systematic anaesthesia medication carts, accurate drug preparation, clear labelling, safe administration, strict infection control, detailed documentation, and responsible drug disposal.

2. **Human errors in preparation and administration can be prevented** with understanding of drug pharmacology, adhering to safety medication use protocols, and maintaining comprehensive medication records.

3. **Storage of anaesthesia medications must be systematically organised** to enhance drug identification and minimise mix-ups despite being stored at various locations.

4. **Diligent syringe labelling, patient verification and vigilant anaesthesia healthcare professionals are essential** during medication administration especially in operating rooms.

5. **Standardised protocols of dilution, preparation, and administration should be implemented** when handling vasopressors, inotropes, highly concentrated, and hyperosmolar drugs.

6. **Prevention strategies and management protocols should be established** for complications of extravasation and inadvertent intra-arterial medication administration.

7. **Maintaining clean workspaces and strictly adhering to infection control protocols** during medication preparation and administration is essential for patient safety.

8. **Accurate documentation of administered medications is crucial** for evaluating and planning patient management and mitigating risks of malpractice.

---

## 2.1 Aims of Safe Medication Administration

1. Ensure that the appropriate medication is administered to the designated patient, in the accurate dosage, through the proper route and at the designated time. **This follows the 5Rs concept:**
   - Right patient
   - Right medicine
   - Right dose
   - Right route
   - Right time of administration

2. Document accurately medication details in the anaesthesia record, patient's drug chart, and controlled medication registers in accordance with **Dangerous Drug Act 1952**.

3. Assess patients for anticipated responses to medications and potential adverse effects.

4. Reduce chances for substance misuse and unauthorised distribution.

---

## 2.2 Responsibilities of Anaesthesia Healthcare Professionals

1. Thorough comprehension of medication pharmacology, potential adverse effects, and its management.

2. Complete knowledge of medication prescription and administration systems, including relevant legislation.

3. Document complete records of prescribed medications in the anaesthesia record and patient's drug chart.

4. Understand the human factors that contribute to medication errors and implement proactive strategies to mitigate them.

5. Monitor patients for the desired effects and potential side effects of the drugs.

---

## 2.3 Collaborative Practices with Hospital Pharmacists

1. Engagement with hospital pharmacists to guarantee the availability and proper presentation of medications.

2. Collaborate with pharmacists to ensure proper storage and disposal procedures of medications.

3. Regular auditing of medication handling and reporting of administration errors to drive improvements in safe medication administration.

---

## 2.4 Purchasing Decisions for Anaesthesia Medications

1. Hospital pharmacy purchasing strategies should be based on 'medication purchasing safety' best practice that include assessment of supply chain, source, quality, labelling, packaging, storage and delivery.

2. Involve pharmacists together with anaesthesiologists, pain medicine and critical care physicians in purchasing decisions.

3. Essential anaesthesia medication purchases must be prioritised to ensure uninterrupted supply.

4. **Purchasing decisions should prioritise clear labelling and avoid look-alike presentations** to ensure easy identification of medications. This includes similar drugs with different concentrations.

5. Consider purchasing ready-to-use medications that reduce the necessity for dilution before administration.

6. Utilisation of prefilled and pre-labelled syringes where appropriate to reduce medication errors. Factors affecting this decision are frequency of use and shelf-life.

## 2.5 Preoperative Medication Reconciliation

### Obtain Complete Medication History

a. All prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, supplements, and herbal/alternative medicines the patient is currently taking.

b. Any occurrences of adverse drug-related events.

c. **Drug allergies and intolerances.** This information should be gathered from both the patient interview and a review of their medical records.

### Preoperative Instructions

- Instruct patients to bring all their medications on admission day or day of surgery
- Provide clear instructions on which medications to continue or withhold prior to surgery:

| Action | Drug Examples |
|--------|---------------|
| **Continue** | Beta blockers, statins, proton pump inhibitors |
| **Withhold** | ACE inhibitors, oral hypoglycaemic agents, diuretics, NSAIDs |
| **Specialist consultation required** | Anticoagulants, insulin |

- Clearly document the medication plan in the patient's record so it is visible to all hospital staff
- Consider alternative routes of administration if patient will be nil by mouth pre-operatively
- Monitor for potential complications related to continuing or withholding specific drugs (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, hypotension, arrhythmias, withdrawal syndromes)

## 2.6 Anaesthesia Medication Storage

1. Storage of anaesthesia medications must adhere to regulations specific to institutional practice. **Dangerous and psychotropic drugs must be securely locked**, and usage controlled and recorded in accordance with Dangerous Drug Act 1952.

2. Anaesthesia medications are stored in various workspaces:
   - Resuscitation trolleys
   - Fluid and drug stores
   - Dangerous Drug Act drug cupboards
   - Anaesthesia medication carts

   The storage should be organised systematically with standardisation within and across institutions, including keeping inventory of drugs, practising First In First Out method and ensuring security of storage.

3. Storage of medication is designed to **optimise drug identification and minimise mix-ups**.

4. **Essential medications should be immediately and independently accessible** to anaesthesia healthcare professionals 24 hours a day in sufficient quantities.

5. Access to essential medications in the recovery bay and post anaesthesia care unit (PACU) should be of similar standard to operation rooms.

6. Routine inventory and check for expired medications should be performed.

### Anaesthesia Medication Cart Recommendations

| Recommendation | Details |
|----------------|---------|
| **Categorised arrangement** | Drugs arranged with clear labelling; limit quantity to prevent selection error |
| **Functional grouping** | Medications with same function stored together in same drawers |
| **Risk-based separation** | High-risk, less-frequently used medications separate from routine drugs |
| **First drawer** | Routine drugs: antiemetics, reversal agents, vasopressors |
| **Second drawer** | High-risk drugs: inotropes, vasodilators, electrolytes |
| **LASA flagging** | Look-alike, sound-alike medications prominently flagged or stored separately |
| **Concentration separation** | Medications with different concentrations clearly segregated and flagged (e.g., local anaesthetics) |
| **Secure locking** | Preferably keyless locks (pin codes or swipe cards) for authorised access only |
| **Regular evaluation** | Account for changes in medication suppliers and ampoule presentations |

## 2.7 Medication Preparation and Verification

1. Prescriptions must specify the **generic drug name, dose, route, frequency, and any special instructions**. This includes prescription to pharmacy and recording in anaesthesia records.

2. Maintain adequate lighting and minimise distractions during medication preparation.

3. **Verify the drug name, concentration, and expiry date** before medication preparation.

4. Standardise workflows for labelling syringes. **Syringes should be labelled immediately after each medication preparation.**

5. Use gauze to hold ampoule while breaking, ensuring breaking part faces away. Use ampoule breakers if available.

6. **Glass particle contamination risk**: Use blunt filter needles or straws with 5-micron filter to aspirate medication from glass ampoules.

7. Check labels on both ampoule and syringe after completion to ensure they match.

8. **Discard and restart the preparation process if interrupted.**

9. Medications prepared for routes other than intravenous should be clearly flagged and separated.

10. Isolate medications for emergency use to avoid unintended administration.

11. **Retain empty ampoules until end of anaesthesia** for verification in case of: no expected drug effect, adverse drug effects, or drug administration errors.

12. Reconstituted medications must be stored according to manufacturer's recommendations. Generally not advisable to keep at room temperature >24 hours.

13. **Multi-dose vials are not recommended** in anaesthesia practice. If unavoidable, proper storage and infection control measures per manufacturer recommendations.

---

## 2.8 Medication Labelling

1. Label syringes according to national standard: **Guideline on Syringe Labelling in Critical Care Areas**.

2. **Reconstituted medication labels must include:**
   - Medication name
   - Concentration
   - Date of preparation
   - Name of person who prepared it (exception: bolus drugs in OTs)

3. Optimise label legibility on ampoules and syringes.

4. **Use colour-coded, self-adhesive pre-printed syringe labels** conforming to ASTM D4774-94 and ISO 26825:2008:
   - Process yellow C for induction agents
   - Fluorescent red 805 for muscle relaxants
   - Blue 297 for narcotics

### Tall Man Lettering (TML) for Look-Alike Drug Names

| Non-TML Labelling | TML Labelling |
|-------------------|---------------|
| Fentanyl / Sufentanil | **FentaNYL** / **SUFentanil** |
| Atracurium / Rocuronium | **ATRAcurium** / **ROcuronium** |
| Ephedrine / Phenylephrine | **EPHEDrine** / **PHENylephrine** |
| Epinephrine / Norepinephrine | **EPINEPHrine** / **NORepinephrine** |
| Dopamine / Dobutamine | **DOPamine** / **DOBUTamine** |
| Ropivacaine / Bupivacaine | **ROPIvacaine** / **BUPIvacaine** |

5. **Handwritten labels or writing on syringes with permanent markers are strongly discouraged.**

6. Sterile labels should be available for sterile procedures. In absence of sterile labels, use different syringe sizes for different medications with vigilance.

7. **High alert medications should have 'HIGH ALERT MEDICATION' labels** on storage shelves, containers, packages and loose vials/ampoules.

8. Short or simple labels during emergency/resuscitation are acceptable to improve speed.

9. **Distinguishable labels on tubing sets are mandatory** for continuous neuraxial and peripheral nerve infusions.

## 2.9 Medication Administration

### 2.9.1 Introduction

a. Good medication administration practice requires **independent double-checks** to verify medications before administration.

b. Operation theatre workflow may make this impractical. Hence, **vigilance must be practised**.

c. Anaesthesia healthcare professionals must be aware that **fatigue may reduce vigilance** and seek assistance to avoid medication error.

d. Utilising **barcode drug identification scanning systems** prior to medication administration can mitigate this issue.

### 2.9.2 Safe Practice

a. **Positively identify every patient** before administering medications.

b. Minimise the time between medication preparation and administration.

c. The anaesthesiologist in charge is responsible for administering medication. If a second professional needs to administer (emergency/relief duty), **clear communication regarding medications is required** or administration under direct supervision.

d. Drugs brought from operation room to recovery bay or ICU for postoperative use should be **labelled and handed over properly** to receiving teams.

e. The 'time-out' session prior to procedures in **WHO Safe Surgery Saves Lives checklist** includes:
   - Patient's drug allergies
   - Timely prophylactic antibiotic administration

f. Administration of highly concentrated drugs, electrolytes, glucose, and insulin requires special attention to ensure medication safety.

## 2.10 Administration of Highly Concentrated Drugs, Electrolytes, Glucose, and Insulin

### Vasopressors (e.g., epinephrine, norepinephrine)

**Inadvertent administration of undiluted drugs can lead to life-threatening complications:**
- Severe hypertension
- Arrhythmias
- Cardiorespiratory arrest

### Highly Concentrated Anaesthetic Drugs (remifentanil, dexmedetomidine)

**Inadvertent administration of undiluted drugs can cause:**
- Severe hypotension
- Severe bradycardia
- Cardiorespiratory arrest

### Concentrated Electrolyte Solutions (potassium chloride, magnesium sulphate)

Errors can lead to:
- Arrhythmias
- Cardiac arrest
- Neurological damage

### Glucose Solutions

Different concentrations administered for hypoglycaemia. Inappropriate administration can cause:
- Electrolyte disturbances
- Osmotic shifts
- Neurological complications

### Insulin

Incorrect administration can cause:
- Severe hypoglycaemia
- Seizures
- Death

Errors occur due to incorrect calculations or failure to monitor blood glucose adequately.

### Safety Measures for High-Concentration Drugs

| Measure | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Separate storage** | Store in clearly labelled compartments or drawers |
| **Pre-filled syringes** | Use when possible |
| **Standardised dilution** | Employ standardised protocols to prevent dilution errors |
| **Double-check** | Always verify concentration, dose, and volume before administration |
| **Independent verification** | Electrolytes require independent double-check by another provider |
| **Colour-coded labels** | Use to differentiate drugs |
| **Slow administration** | Administer slowly (infusion) and titrate to effect |
| **Central venous access** | Vasopressors, concentrated electrolytes and glucose should be via central venous catheter. Initial peripheral administration via large bore IV acceptable while waiting for central access |

## Table 2: Medications to be Administered via Central Venous Catheter

| Medication | Recommendation |
|------------|----------------|
| **Adenosine** | Injection into most proximal injection site or central venous line |
| **Amiodarone** | Administer centrally if available. Central line recommended for infusions >24 hours and concentrations >2mg/ml |
| **Calcium Gluconate** | High risk for tissue necrosis. Administer slowly via central line or large peripheral vein |
| **Dantrolene** | Vesicant. Ensure proper dilution as per guideline |
| **Dextrose in water** | Central line preferred for concentrations ≥10% |
| **Dobutamine** | Central line preferred. Peripheral may be used <4 hours while preparing central line. Use large bore IV at rate <2 mcg/kg/min |
| **Dopamine** | Central line preferred. May use 1600 mcg/ml at doses ≤3 mcg/kg/min peripherally. Central line for higher concentrations or infusions >12 hours |
| **Epinephrine** | Central line preferred. Peripheral via large bore IV may be used <4 hours while preparing central access |
| **Esmolol** | Boluses via large bore IV. Central line preferred for concentrations >10mg/ml or administration >72 hours |
| **Mannitol** | Boluses via large bore peripheral IV. Central line still recommended if possible. Central preferred for infusion |
| **Norepinephrine** | Central line preferred. Peripheral via large bore IV may be used <4 hours while preparing central access |
| **Phenylephrine** | Peripheral via large bore IV for boluses. Central line preferred for infusion |
| **Phenytoin** | Avoid extravasation - severe tissue necrosis may occur. Administer through large vein (at least antecubital) with catheter ≥20 gauge |
| **Potassium salts** | Max peripheral rate: 10 mEq/hour. Central line required for concentrations >0.1 mEq/ml |
| **3% Sodium Chloride** | Administer hypertonic saline only via large bore IV or preferably central venous catheter |
| **Thiopental** | Boluses via peripheral line. Infusion ≥5 mg/kg via central line |
| **Vasopressin** | Central line preferred. Peripheral via large bore IV may be used <4 hours while preparing central access |

> **Important:** This table is critical for safe administration of high-risk medications. When central access is not immediately available, use large bore peripheral IV temporarily while establishing central venous access.

## 2.11 Intravenous Medication Delivery

1. **Standardise the use of intravenous infusion pumps and syringe drivers** within each facility to improve familiarity and reduce error.

2. Place the label at the **patient-end of the infusion line** for fast identification of the medication.

3. **Anti-siphon and anti-reflux (one-way) valves are recommended** to prevent free flow and retrograde pumping of analgesic agent into infusion lines. PCA delivery lines **must utilise** infusion tubing with anti-siphon and anti-reflux valves.

4. Infusion line connections should utilise a **lock design that minimises disconnection risk**. Checking for disconnection is the responsibility of anaesthesia healthcare professionals especially when intended drug effect is not seen.

5. **Smart infusion pumps with dose error reduction software are highly recommended**. However, professionals need to understand pump limitations including intervariability among patients.

## 2.12 Management of Extravasation Injuries and Intra-Arterial Injection

### 2.12.1 Background

Extravasation injuries and inadvertent intra-arterial drug injection may occur during anaesthesia practice due to:
- Higher number of intravenous drugs administered
- Some drugs are vesicants, irritants and hyperosmolar solutions

### Table 3: Complications of Extravasation and Intra-Arterial Injection

| Drug | Extravasation Complications | Intra-Arterial Injection Complications |
|------|----------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Atracurium** | Tissue ischaemia and necrosis | Tissue ischaemia |
| **Ketamine** | Tissue ischaemia and necrosis | Tissue necrosis |
| **Propofol** | Tissue ischaemia | 'Distal' blanching and hyperaemia |
| **Rocuronium** | Local irritation | Tissue ischaemia |
| **Thiopental** | Tissue ischaemia and necrosis | Tissue ischaemia and necrosis |

**Other high-risk drugs:**
- Hyperosmolar solutions: calcium chloride, sodium bicarbonate
- Acidic or alkaline drugs: amiodarone, phenytoin
- Inotropes: norepinephrine, vasopressin

**Sequelae:**
- Minor extravasation effects are usually minor
- Major complications: tissue necrosis, tissue loss, scarring
- Intra-arterial injection: extremity ischaemia and gangrene

### 2.12.6 Steps to Prevent Extravasation Injuries

a. Be cautious with IV lines at antecubital fossa and joints (easier to dislodge with movement)

b. **Flush and check IV line is still in vein before every use**

c. Use secure adhesive dressing and clear dressing to allow observation of swelling or inflammation

d. Educate patients to report pain, stinging, or burning sensation on injection/infusion

e. Remove promptly lines dislodged from vein

f. Aspirate central line lumens to confirm not migrated before every use

### 2.12.7 Management of Extravasation Injuries

a. **Stop drug administration**, attempt to aspirate 5-10 ml from IV line, remove IV line

b. Assess severity using Milliam assessment grading system. Mark edges of extravasation for daily assessment

c. Elevate affected limb, apply warm or cold compression accordingly, provide analgesia

d. **Refer to plastic surgeon** if: high risk drugs (vesicants/hyperosmolar), Grade 3-4 severity, or signs of compartment syndrome

e. Inform patient of incident and management plans

f. Document assessment, management, and patient/relative counselling

g. Consider informing hospital risk management team for severe cases

### 2.12.8 Steps to Prevent Intra-Arterial Injection

a. Be cautious with high-risk areas near arteries (femoral, antecubital fossa)

b. When signs of arterial cannulation appear, exclude arterial placement or remove line

c. **Alert signs of intra-arterial injection:**
   - Severe pain on injection
   - Pallor
   - Paraesthesia
   - Hyperaemia
   - Cyanosis of affected limb

   **Stop drug administration immediately** and check line placement

d. **General arterial line precautions:**
   - Use cannula without injection port OR close injection port with dressing
   - Label arterial line and all ports accordingly
   - Use red lining tubing and red injection ports
   - Provide regular education to staff using arterial lines

### 2.12.9 Management of Intra-Arterial Injection

a. Stop drug administration and assess limb using tissue ischaemia score

b. **Leave line for angiogram and intra-arterial therapy if needed**. Remove within 48 hours to prevent clot formation

c. Provide analgesia and elevate affected limb

d. Monitor perfusion to affected limb

e. Consider interventions if ischaemia developing and high-risk drugs administered:
   - Anti-coagulant therapy
   - Angiography and intra-arterial thrombolysis
   - Vasodilator drugs

f. **Refer to vascular surgeon and interventional radiologist**

g. Inform patient and document comprehensively

h. Consider informing hospital risk management team

## 2.13 Infection Control in Medication Administration

1. Maintain clean workspaces and adhere to **Guidelines on Infection Control in Anaesthesia**. Good practices:
   - Recapping syringes with caps or plugs
   - Keeping syringes in dedicated medication trays
   - Proper hand hygiene while handling medications

2. **Use single-dose vials or ampoules** for each patient. If sharing unavoidable, ensure integrity of contents and prevent cross-contamination.

3. Between patients, **decontaminate all work surfaces and medication trays**. Using different trays for each patient is advisable.

4. **Discard all syringes after completion of each case**. Sharing used syringes is not acceptable.

5. Crystalloid solutions used as diluent may be cross-contaminated when used for multiple patients. Use only clean unused syringes to draw diluent. **Hub disinfection with 70% alcohol swab** required prior to each use.

6. **IV bolus medication with needle carries needle prick injury risk** - highly discouraged. Inject directly into IV access port.

7. Keep IV access ports clean and covered with cap when not in use. Hub disinfection with 70% alcohol swab and allowing to dry is good practice. Passive hub disinfection is another option.

## 2.15 Medication Documentation

1. **Accurately record each medication administered**, including:
   - Drug name
   - Dose
   - Route
   - Time
   - Date

2. The anaesthesia record must be **signed by the anaesthesiologist** and include:
   - Full name
   - Malaysian Medical Council (MMC) registration number
   - Use of doctor identification stamps is highly encouraged

3. Ensure **legibility of medication records**, especially handwritten documentation. Use of electronic records enhances accuracy.

4. **Document handling of controlled substances** according to regulatory requirements. Records must include disposal of leftover controlled substances.
