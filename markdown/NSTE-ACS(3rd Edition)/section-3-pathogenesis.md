# SECTION 3: PATHOGENESIS
## CPG MANAGEMENT OF NON-ST ELEVATION MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION (NSTE-ACS), 3RD EDITION, 2021

ACS is commonly due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture, fissure or ulceration with superimposed thrombosis and coronary vasospasm. Depending on the acuteness of onset and the degree of occlusion and the presence of collaterals, patients can present as NSTE-ACS or STEMI. In NSTE-ACS the thrombus is either non-occlusive or there is complete thrombosis of a vessel that is well collateralised.

The majority (66%–78%) of ACS arise from lesions with <50% stenosis and less than 5% arise from lesions exhibiting >70% stenosis. From postmortem studies and in vivo studies using intravascular ultrasound, four pathologic pathways to ACS have been postulated:

1. **Plaque rupture** — The most common cause. It involves disruption of a thin fibrous cap overlying a large lipid-rich necrotic core, leading to exposure of thrombogenic material to the blood stream and subsequent thrombus formation.
2. **Plaque erosion** — Involves endothelial denudation without rupture of the fibrous cap. It is more common in younger patients, women, smokers, and in those with hypertriglyceridaemia.
3. **Calcified nodule** — A less common cause involving eruptive calcified nodules protruding into the vessel lumen.
4. **Coronary artery dissection** — Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD), which is an important cause of ACS especially in younger women.

MI can be classified as 5 types depending on the pathology, clinical features, prognosis and treatment strategies (Table 6). This CPG focuses on NSTE-ACS which is usually either:

- **Type 1 MI** — Spontaneous MI related to atherosclerotic plaque rupture, with ulceration, fissuring, erosion or dissection.
- **Type 2 MI** — Often due to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and/or demand. It may occur in the presence of coronary atherosclerosis without plaque rupture or in the absence of atherosclerosis. This is an important cause of ACS in the elderly.

### Overlapping Content: Definition of MI Types (from Section 2)

**Type 1: Spontaneous MI due to coronary athero-thrombosis**
Spontaneous MI related to atherosclerotic plaque rupture, ulceration, fissuring, erosion, or dissection with resulting intraluminal thrombus leading to decreased myocardial blood flow or distal platelet emboli with ensuing myocyte necrosis.

**Type 2: MI secondary to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen demand and supply**
MI that occurs due to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and/or demand. It may occur in the presence of coronary atherosclerosis without plaque rupture or in the absence of atherosclerosis e.g., coronary endothelial dysfunction, coronary artery spasm, coronary embolism, coronary artery dissection, tachy/bradyarrhythmias, anaemia, respiratory failure, sepsis, hypotension, and hypertension with or without LVH.

**Type 3: MI resulting in death when biomarker values are unavailable**
Cardiac death with symptoms suggestive of myocardial ischaemia and presumed new ischaemic ECG changes or new LBBB, but death occurring before blood samples could be obtained.

**Type 4a: MI related to PCI**
MI associated with PCI defined by elevation of cTn values 5x > 99th percentile URL in patients with normal baseline values or a rise of cTn values > 20% if baseline values are elevated but stable or falling.

**Type 4b: MI related to stent thrombosis**
MI associated with stent thrombosis detected by coronary angiography or autopsy in the setting of myocardial ischaemia.

**Type 5: MI related to CABG**
MI associated with CABG defined by elevation of cTn values 10x > 99th percentile URL in patients with normal baseline cTn values.
