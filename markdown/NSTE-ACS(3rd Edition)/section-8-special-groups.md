# SECTION 8: NSTE-ACS IN SPECIAL GROUPS
## CPG MANAGEMENT OF NON-ST ELEVATION MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION (NSTE-ACS), 3RD EDITION, 2021

---

## 8.1 NSTE-ACS in Older Persons

Age is a powerful risk factor for CVD and also an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes after CVD events, for complications after cardiovascular procedures and interventions, and for side effects of pharmacotherapy. International registries show that 32% to 43% of NSTE-ACS, and about 24%–28% of STEMI admissions were for patients aged ≥ 75 years.

These older persons with NSTE-ACS are more likely to be women, have lower body mass indices, higher prevalence of such comorbidities as hypertension, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, TIA/stroke, anaemia and renal insufficiency.

The mortality rate after a first non-STEMI in the oldest old patients is highest: among patients who were 65–79, 80–84, 85–89, and at least 90 years old, mortality increased progressively from 13.3% to 23.6%, 33.6%, and 45.5%, respectively.

### 8.1.1 Clinical Presentation

A high index of suspicion is necessary to make a diagnosis of NSTE-ACS in older patients. Only 40% of those aged >85 years had chest pain on presentation compared with 77% of those aged <65 years. Older patients were more likely to present with:

- Dyspnoea (49%)
- Diaphoresis (26%)
- Nausea and vomiting (24%)
- Neurological symptoms such as acute confusional states and syncope (19%)

Acute pulmonary oedema is a common presentation of NSTE-ACS in the older person.

Type 2 MI is also common in this age group, NSTE-ACS occurring in the setting of another acute illness e.g. tachycardia, pneumonia, sepsis, bleeding episodes.

ECGs are often non-diagnostic and serial ECGs are recommended to detect evolving changes.

Cardiac troponins are commonly elevated > 99th percentile URL in older patients presenting without ACS or other acute illnesses known to cause troponin elevation. In one study almost 40% of patients aged > 70 years had elevated troponins. The conventional cut-off value (99th percentile: 0.014 ng/mL) provided low specificity, particularly in older adults.

### 8.1.2 Management

**When managing the older patient, one should consider the biological age rather than the chronological age.**

#### 8.1.2.1 Pharmacotherapy

- Older persons should receive the same standard pharmacotherapy as younger patients, but with dose adjustments as needed.
- Renal function should be assessed and doses of renally excreted drugs adjusted accordingly.
- Antiplatelet therapy: Clopidogrel is preferred over prasugrel and ticagrelor in very elderly patients due to lower bleeding risk.
- Bleeding risk is higher and should be carefully weighed against thrombotic risk.
- The PRECISE-DAPT score is particularly useful in this population.

#### 8.1.2.2 Revascularization

- Older persons have greater in-hospital and long-term benefits with an early invasive strategy. However, there is an increased risk of major bleeding.
- Age alone should not be a contraindication for an invasive strategy.
- The decision should be individualised, taking into account frailty, cognitive status, comorbidities, and patient preferences.

#### 8.1.2.3 Cardiac Rehabilitation

- All eligible older patients should be referred for cardiac rehabilitation.
- Programs should be tailored to the individual's functional capacity and comorbidities.

## 8.2 NSTE-ACS in Women

### 8.2.1 Clinical Presentation

- Women with ACS are more likely to present with atypical symptoms compared to men.
- Women are more likely to present with NSTE-ACS rather than STEMI.
- Delays in presentation and diagnosis are more common in women.

### 8.2.2 Management

- Women should be managed with the same pharmacological therapy as that for men for acute care and for secondary prevention.
- Women who are low risk and cTn negative should be treated medically.
- Women who are cTn positive should be considered for an invasive strategy.
- A conservative strategy is recommended for women who are stabilised and remain biomarker negative. [Grade IIa, Level A]

## 8.3 NSTE-ACS in Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

### 8.3.1 Diagnosis

- Cardiac troponins are frequently elevated in patients with CKD, even in the absence of ACS.
- Serial measurements with assessment of rise and/or fall pattern are essential for diagnosis.
- The same diagnostic criteria apply as for patients with normal renal function.

### 8.3.2 Management

- In general, patients with CKD should be managed in a similar manner as those with normal renal function.
- They however have a higher bleeding tendency and doses of medications need to be adjusted according to the renal function.
- Dose adjustments are required for:
  - **Enoxaparin:** 1 mg/kg once daily (instead of twice daily) if CrCl < 30 mL/min.
  - **Fondaparinux:** Contraindicated if CrCl < 20 mL/min.
  - **Prasugrel and Ticagrelor:** No dose adjustment required.
  - **Clopidogrel:** No dose adjustment required.
- Contrast-induced nephropathy should be prevented by adequate hydration before and after coronary angiography.
- An invasive strategy should be considered in patients with moderate-to-severe CKD, as they derive significant benefit. However, the bleeding risk is higher.

### Overlapping Content: Key Recommendations 11 (from Section 1) — Discharge Medications

All patients with NSTE-ACS should be on optimal medical therapy at discharge. This includes:
- DAPT with aspirin + clopidogrel (or ticagrelor or prasugrel).
- AND high intensity statins to achieve LDL-C target of < 1.8 mmol/L (preferably < 1.4 mmol/L).
- AND if angina/ischaemia is present, ß-blockers and/or CCBs as first-line; ivabradine, trimetazidine, long-acting nitrates and ranolazine as add-on.
- AND if LVEF < 40% and heart failure, ß-blockers, ACEIs/ARBs and aldosterone receptor antagonists. SGLT2-inhibitors can be instituted in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.
