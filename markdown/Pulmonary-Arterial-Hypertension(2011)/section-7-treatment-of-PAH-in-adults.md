## 7. Treatment of PAH in Adults

The aim of therapy in patients with PAH is to improve survival, disease-related symptoms and QoL. Treatment can be classified as conventional therapy and targeted PAH-specific therapy.

## 7.1. Conventional Treatment
Conventional treatment options include oxygen therapy in cases of hypoxaemia, anticoagulants such as warfarin, and digoxin with diuretics in cases of right-sided heart failure.

### 7.1.1. Oxygen
**[Level C]** Oxygen can be used in patients who have nocturnal hypoxaemia due to mild hypoventilation. Desaturation during sleep occurs in the early mornings and can be associated with syncope and seizures. Some patients with severe PAH develop hypoxaemia and may benefit from supplemental oxygen. Those with PO₂ < 60mmHg should be considered for long term oxygen therapy (LTOT).

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Supplemental oxygen should be considered in PAH patients with hypoxaemia.

### 7.1.2. Anticoagulation
**[Level C]** In the absence of contraindications, anticoagulation is recommended as a part of the general treatment regimen to decrease the likelihood of thromboembolic complications. Warfarin is the anticoagulant of choice and the International Normalised Ratio (INR) should be maintained between 2 to 3. However, for IPAH patients with a higher risk of bleeding, the target INR should be 1.5 to 2.5.

Retrospective series show warfarin improves 3-year survival from ~21-31% up to 47-49%, and 5-year survival up to 62%. For patients with CTEPH, adequate anticoagulation is important to prevent further thromboembolism.

**Recommendation [Grade II]:** Anticoagulation should be considered in IPAH patients unless contraindicated.

### 7.1.3. Digoxin
**[Level C]** Digoxin has been shown to improve cardiac output acutely in IPAH, although its efficacy is unknown when administered chronically. It may be useful in patients with atrial fibrillation and in PAH patients with heart failure who remain symptomatic on medical therapy.

**Recommendation [Grade II-b]:** Digoxin can be considered in PAH patients with heart failure with or without atrial fibrillation.

### 7.1.4. Diuretics
**[Level C]** In patients with right heart failure secondary to PAH, increased filling pressure can further distend an already dilated right ventricle, which in turn can worsen function and decrease cardiac output. Therefore, although no RCTs exist, decreasing right ventricular preload with the aid of diuretics is the mainstay of treatment for patients with right heart failure.

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Diuretics should be given to PAH patients with right heart failure.

### 7.1.5. Calcium Channel Blockers
**[Level C]** If, during RHC, the pulmonary vasoreactive test is positive, the treatment of choice is high-dose CCBs (up to 240 mg/day of nifedipine or up to 900 mg/day of diltiazem), as improved survival with long-term use has been demonstrated in this cohort of patients. If CCBs demonstrate no clinical improvement after one month or are unable to achieve **[WHO Class I]** or **[WHO Class II]** with associated improvement in haemodynamics over three months, then patients should be treated as non-responders and PAH-specific therapies considered.

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Only patients who demonstrate a positive vasoreactive response should be treated with high dose CCBs.

### 7.1.6. Vaccination
**[Level C]** Annual influenza and pneumococcal pneumonia vaccination are recommended in patients with PAH. Patients with PAH who develop pneumonia should be treated early and appropriately. Dehydration and vasodilatation during infection should be avoided and/or adequately treated.

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Patients with PAH should receive influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations.

### 7.1.7. Avoid Pregnancy
**[Level C]** Pregnancy in patients with PAH is associated with a high risk of maternal death. PAH is a contraindication to pregnancy and an appropriate method of birth control is highly recommended in women with childbearing potential. Should the patient become pregnant, she should be informed of the high risk of pregnancy, and advised termination of pregnancy. For those who insist on continuing pregnancy, management requires close collaboration between obstetricians and the PAH team and disease-targeted therapies.

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Patients with PAH must avoid pregnancy.

### 7.1.8. Physical Activities
**[Level B]** Patients should be encouraged to be active as their symptoms allow. However, excessive physical exertion leading to distressing symptoms should be avoided. A recent study demonstrated an improvement in exercise capacity in patients who took part in a training programme.

**Recommendation [Grade II-a]:** Appropriate physical activity is encouraged.

### 7.1.9. QoL and Non-Pharmacological Treatment
PAH is a chronic, life-shortening disease and many patients suffer from limitations in their physical mobility, energy, emotional reactions and social isolation. Understandably, many patients are affected by a degree of anxiety and/or depression that can have a profound impact on their QoL. Assisting patients to adapt to the uncertainty associated with their illness is important, as is referral to psychologists or psychiatrists when needed. Support groups for patients, families and carers are useful in improving the understanding and the acceptance of the disease.

## 7.2. PAH-Specific Therapy

In Malaysia, therapeutic options for patients classified with **[WHO Class II]**, **[WHO Class III]** or **[WHO Class IV]** disease include bosentan, ambrisentan, iloprost and sildenafil. The following is a summary of the evidence from clinical trials performed with these agents.

### 7.2.1. Bosentan
**[Level A]** Bosentan is an oral dual endothelin receptor antagonist (ERA). It mediates the vasoconstrictive and mitogenic effects of ET-1. 

Bosentan improves exercise capacity, **[WHO Class II]** and **[WHO Class III]** functional status, cardiopulmonary haemodynamics, QoL,  echocardiographic variables and time to clinical worsening  compared with placebo and conventional therapy.  A summary of patient characteristics and results from RCTs with bosentan versus placebo is presented in Appendix 1: Patient Characteristics from RCTs with Bosentan vs. Placebo and Appendix 2: Results from RCTs with Bosentan vs. Placebo.

Long-term survival data shows a 2-year Kaplan-Meier survival estimate of 89%, and a 3-year survival of 79%. Annual mortality in registries is observed at 11.8% for IPAH and 16.6% for SSc-PAH. A summary of patient characteristics and results from long-term extension studies and registries with bosentan is presented in Appendix 3: Patient Characteristics from Long-Term Extension Studies and Registries with Bosentan and Appendix 4: Results from Long-Term Extension Studies and Registries with Bosentan. 

The recommended dosage is 62.5 mg bd to 125 mg bd. Common side effects include hepatic dysfunction, headache, flushing, lower limb oedema, palpitation, and dyspepsia. The most notable side effect is liver aminotransferase elevations (occurring in ~7.6% to 12.8% of patients, with a 3.2% discontinuation rate). Liver function monitoring is strictly recommended in all patients receiving ERAs.These results suggest that elevated liver aminotransferases can be effectively managed in the majority of patients receiving bosentan.

### 7.2.2. Ambrisentan
**[Level B]** Ambrisentan is a selective ET-A receptor antagonist approved for **[WHO Class II]** and **[WHO Class III]**. Current approved dose is 5mg daily which can be increased to 10mg daily. Incidence of abnormal LFTs ranges from 0.8 to 3%. In a small group of patients who were intolerant to either bosentan due to LFTs abnormalities, ambrisentan at a dose of 5mg was well tolerated However, patients treated with ambrisentan require regular LFTs. Ambrisentan has been associated with increased incidence of peripheral oedema.

### 7.2.3. Iloprost
**[Level B]** Iloprost is a prostacyclin derivative approved by the FDA in 2004 for **[WHO Class III]** and **[WHO Class IV]** PAH. It is a potent vasodilator affecting both pulmonary and systemic circulation, and currently the only commercially available prostanoid agent in Malaysia.

Administered via an ultrasonic nebuliser with a half-life of 20 to 25 minutes. Chronic use requires 6 to 9 inhalations a day to obtain a sustained clinical benefit. The recommended inhaled dose is 2.5-5 μg/inhalation. Common side effects include cough, headache, flushing, and jaw pain.

Short-term RCT data shows an increase in exercise capacity, an improvement in PVR, and a reduction in clinical events for PAH and CTEPH patients. Long-term studies report sustained benefits in exercise capacity and haemodynamics at 1 year, and event-free (death, transplantation, switch to i.v. therapy, or addition of other active oral therapy) survival rates of 53%, 29%, and 20% at 1-, 2-, and 3-years, respectively. Patient characteristics and results from these studies are presented in Appendix 5: Patient Characteristics and Results from RCTs with Inhaled Iloprost vs. Placebo and Appendix 6: Patient Characteristics and Results from Long-Term Extension Studies with Inhaled Iloprost.

### 7.2.4. Sildenafil
**[Level A]** Sildenafil is an oral PDE-5 inhibitor. It increases the activity of endogenous nitric oxide, inducing relaxation and anti-proliferative effects on vascular smooth muscle cells.

RCTs with Sildenafil as monotherapy versus placebo (doses ranging from 60 to 240 mg/day) demonstrate that sildenafil reduces mPAP and PVR, increases cardiac index (CI), and significantly improves 6MWD compared to placebo. Higher doses show greater improvements in mPAP, CI, PVR, and functional class. Results from RCTs are presented in Appendix 7: Patient Characteristics from RCTs with Sildenafil vs. Placebo and Appendix 8: Results from RCTs with Sildenafil vs. Placebo.

Long-term efficacy data (based on an 80 mg tds dose) reports a one-year survival rate of 96% (Appendix 9: Patient Characteristics and Results from Long-Term Extension Studies with Sildenafil). The FDA-approved dose is 20 mg tds administered orally. However, higher doses (up to 80 mg tds) may be more efficacious. Common side effects include headache, flushing, dyspepsia, epistaxis, nasal congestion, and impaired vision.

### 7.2.5. Tadalafil
**[Level B]** Tadalafil is a long-acting PDE-5 inhibitor. RCT data on PAH patients (including those on background bosentan therapy) has shown favourable results on exercise capacity, symptoms, haemodynamics, and time to clinical worsening at the 40 mg daily dose. Side effects are similar to sildenafil (headache, flushing, dyspepsia, epistaxis, nasal congestion, and impaired vision).

**Recommendations:**
1. **[Grade I]** Patients with symptomatic iPAH should be started on PAH specific therapy.
2. **[Grade II-a]** PAH secondary to CTD or CHD can be considered for PAH specific therapy.
3. **[Grade I]** The choice of a PAH specific drug includes an ERA, inhaled Iloprost, or a PDE-5 inhibitor.

## 7.3. PAH-Specific Therapy and Survival

Due to the short-term nature of RCTs (12-16 weeks), survival is rarely an endpoint. To date, only the prostacyclin analogue epoprostenol has demonstrated a survival benefit compared to conventional therapy. Epoprostenol is FDA-approved for IPAH in **[WHO Class III]** and **[WHO Class IV]**, and recommended first-line for patients with **[WHO Class IV]** symptoms, though complex administration limits its use.

Longer-term open-label series with active treatments (epoprostenol and bosentan) report 1- and 2-year survival rates of 85% to 97% and 70% to 91%, respectively. This is a dramatic contrast to historical natural history without specific therapy (1981 NIH registry), which had a median life expectancy of 2.8 years and 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 68%, 48%, and 34%. Furthermore, meta-analysis of PAH RCTs suggests that active treatments are associated with a 43% reduction in mortality (relative risk: 0.57; 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.92; p=0.023).

## 7.4. Evaluation of Response to Treatment

Patients should be evaluated clinically based on symptoms (WHO functional class), signs (right heart failure), and parameters such as 6MWD, right ventricular function (via echo), natriuretic peptide, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and haemodynamics (via RHC). Refer to Table 14 for suggested parameters and targets. 

## Table 14: Parameters and Targets for Re-evaluation (Follow Up Assessment)
| Parameters | Target |
|---|---|
| Functional class | Improvement by 3 months, ideally to **[WHO Class I or II]** status |
| 6-min walk distance | > 380 m |
| Cardiopulmonary exercise testing | Peak oxygen consumption > 10.4 ml/kg/min |
| Biomarkers (BNP, NT-proBNP) | Reducing trend from baseline |
| Echocardiography (RV function, TAPSE, pericardial effusion) | Improvement in parameters |
| Right heart catheterization | Right atrial pressure < 10 mmHg; Cardiac index > 2.5 L/min/m² |

Repeat testing is useful to assess response. Patients with a satisfactory response can be continued on treatment and followed up regularly. Patients with an unsatisfactory response should be considered for combination therapy or other interventions, including lung transplant.

**Recommendation:** The response to treatment should be monitored in patients who are on a PAH specific drug.

## 7.5. Combination Therapy

**[Level B]** Combination therapy is an attractive option with the development of several therapeutic classes of agents with different mechanisms of action and potential synergy. The goal of combination therapy is to maximise therapeutic efficacy while limiting toxicity and potential drug-drug interactions. It involves either initiation with 2 or more concomitant PAH-specific therapies (especially for **[WHO Class IV]** patients due to poor prognosis), or more commonly, sequential administration following clinical deterioration on a first-line agent.

Sequential add-on therapies achieving successful endpoints include various combinations (e.g., bosentan + epoprostenol/iloprost/beraprost/sildenafil; sildenafil + epoprostenol/iloprost/beraprost/trepostinil).

The most common combination strategies:
1. Addition of a second oral drug (e.g., sildenafil + ERA or ERA + sildenafil) – the most common combination for **[WHO Class III]** patients. It provides additional clinical benefits like improved exercise capacity, increased time to clinical deterioration, and improved haemodynamics compared to monotherapy. *Note:* Significant pharmacologic interactions exist (bosentan decreases maximum plasma concentration of sildenafil by 55%; sildenafil increases bosentan concentration by 42%), requiring careful monitoring.
2. Prostanoid + sildenafil
3. Prostanoid + ERA

For patients in **[WHO Class IV]**, starting with two or more targeted therapies simultaneously can be considered in view of the poor prognosis and likelihood of failure of monotherapy.

There are a number of well-designed studies ongoing which should help determine the potential benefits of a variety of combination therapies in Appendix 10: Clinical Trials in Progress with Combination Therapy. Individuals with PAH who demonstrate an inadequate response to monotherapy should be considered for a combination of two or more diseasetargeted therapies.

**Recommendation [Grade II-b]:** Patients with PAH who have an inadequate response to monotherapy using a PAH specific drug, should be considered for sequential combination therapy.

## 7.6. Atrial Septostomy

**[Level C]** Atrial septostomy creates a right-to-left inter-atrial shunt to decrease right heart filling pressures and improve left heart filling. It serves mainly as a palliative bridge to lung transplantation (successful bridging rates of 30% to 40%), improving haemodynamics and clinical symptoms.

Current evidence suggests a benefit in **[WHO Class IV]** patients with right heart failure refractory to optimal medical therapy, or those with severe syncopal symptoms. The preferred choice is graded balloon atrial septostomy, which must only be performed in experienced designated centres with an active thoracic transplant programme. 

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Atrial septostomy is reserved for selected patients with refractory symptoms and should be performed in specialized centres.

## 7.7. Transplantation

**[Level C]** Lung and heart-lung transplantation are indicated in PAH patients whose prognosis remains poor despite maximum medical therapy. Early discussion with experienced transplant physicians is recommended, though long waiting times and organ shortages significantly restrict utility.

**Recommendation [Grade I]:** Lung and heart lung transplant is reserved for selected patients with refractory symptoms and should be performed in transplant centres.

## Grades of Recommendation
| GRADE | DEFINITION |
|---|---|
| **I** | Conditions for which there is evidence and/or general agreement that a given procedure/therapy is beneficial, useful and/or effective. |
| **II** | Conditions for which there is conflicting evidence and/or a divergence of opinion about the usefulness/efficacy of a given procedure/therapy. |
| **II-a** | Weight of evidence/opinion is in favor of its usefulness/efficacy. |
| **II-b** | Usefulness/efficacy is less well established by evidence/opinion. |
| **III** | Conditions for which there is evidence and/or general agreement that a procedure/therapy is not useful/effective, and in some cases may be harmful. |

## Levels of Evidence
| LEVEL | DEFINITION |
|---|---|
| **A** | Data derived from multiple randomised clinical trials or meta-analyses. |
| **B** | Data derived from a single randomised clinical trial or large non-randomised studies. |
| **C** | Consensus of opinion of the experts and/or small studies, retrospective studies and registries. |

## Table 2: WHO Classification of Functional Status in Patients with PHT

| Class | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Class I** | Patients with PHT but without resulting limitation of physical activity. Ordinary physical activity does not cause undue dyspnoea or fatigue, chest pain, or near syncope. |
| **Class II** | Patients with PHT resulting in slight limitation of physical activity. They are comfortable at rest. Ordinary physical activity causes undue dyspnoea or fatigue, chest pain, or near syncope. |
| **Class III** | Patients with PHT resulting in marked limitation of physical activity. They are comfortable at rest. Less-than-ordinary physical activity causes undue dyspnoea or fatigue, chest pain, or near syncope. |
| **Class IV** | Patients with PHT with inability to carry out any physical activity without symptoms. These patients manifest signs of right heart failure. Dyspnoea and/or fatigue may even be present at rest. Discomfort is increased by any physical activity. |
