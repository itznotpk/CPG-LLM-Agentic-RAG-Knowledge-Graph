## 2.1: Importance Of Screening

Screening for diabetes in pregnant women is important to identify asymptomatic patients who may have overt diabetes before they develop symptoms such as excessive thirst, urination, or fatigue. Early identification allows timely intervention to reduce maternal and fetal complications.

Two main screening strategies exist:
- **Universal screening:** All pregnant women undergo a screening test for GDM.
- **Selective screening:** Only women with identified risk factors are screened.

[Level I] In a Cochrane systematic review, there was insufficient evidence to determine if screening for GDM and types of screening can improve maternal and fetal health outcomes due to small studies and poor quality evidence. However, one included study showed that universal screening was better than selective screening at detecting GDM (RR=0.44, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.75).

Ideally, universal screening should be adhered to. However, if resources are limited, selective screening is acceptable, focusing on individuals at risk of developing GDM. [Level III]

## 2.2: Risk Factors For GDM

Women at risk of developing GDM include those with any one of the following:
- Body mass index (BMI) >27 kg/m²
- Previous history of GDM
- First-degree relative with diabetes mellitus
- History of macrosomia (birth weight >4 kg)
- Bad obstetric history (unexplained intrauterine death, congenital anomalies such as neural tube defects or cardiac defects, shoulder dystocia)
- Glycosuria ≥2+ on two occasions
- Current obstetric problems (essential hypertension, pregnancy-induced hypertension, polyhydramnios, or current use of corticosteroids)

Increasing age has been associated with an increased risk of developing GDM. Based on consensus between the Review Committee and Development Group, pregnant women aged ≥25 years without other risk factors should have GDM screening at 24–28 weeks. [Level III]

## 2.3: Screening Test — Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)

[Level I] A Cochrane systematic review found insufficient evidence to conclude the best strategy to diagnose GDM. However, the OGTT (75 g) has been recommended by international guidelines as the preferred diagnostic test.

The OGTT should be done:
- At **booking** (as early as possible) for women with any risk factors for GDM.
- Repeated at **24–28 weeks of gestation** if the initial result was negative.

There is insufficient evidence to suggest HbA1c alone as a useful diagnostic test for GDM; it is not an acceptable alternative to OGTT. [Level III]

[Level II-2] In a meta-analysis on screening for GDM, random blood sugar measurement was found to be an inadequate test to screen for GDM.

## 2.4: Diagnostic Criteria For GDM

GDM is diagnosed in the presence of **any one** of the following OGTT results:

| Measurement | Diagnostic Threshold |
|---|---|
| Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) | ≥ 5.1 mmol/L |
| 2-hour postprandial (2-HPP) | ≥ 7.8 mmol/L |

## 2.5: Overt Diabetes In Pregnancy

There is no universally accepted definition of overt diabetes in pregnancy. The diagnosis is made when blood glucose level is high during the first trimester, meeting the criteria below.

Overt diabetes in pregnancy is **suspected** in the presence of at least one of the following:
- FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L
- RPG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with symptoms

**Important:** The diagnosis of overt DM must be **confirmed with a second test** (FPG, RPG, or OGTT) on a separate day. [Level III] A single elevated result is insufficient for diagnosis.

If overt diabetes is confirmed, treatment and follow-up should be carried out **as for pre-existing diabetes**, not as GDM. [Level III]

## 2.6: Recommendation 1

> **[Grade A]** Screening for gestational diabetes mellitus based on risk factors using 75 gram oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) should be done at booking. If the test is negative, it should be repeated at 24–28 weeks of gestation.
>
> **[Grade C]** For women at the age of 25 or more with no other risk factors, OGTT should be done at 24–28 weeks of gestation.
>
> **[Grade C]** Overt diabetes in pregnancy should be managed as pre-existing diabetes.

| Level | Study Design |
|---|---|
| I | Evidence from at least one properly randomised controlled trial |
| II-1 | Evidence obtained from well-designed controlled trials without randomisation |
| II-2 | Evidence obtained from well-designed cohort or case-control analytic studies, preferably from more than one centre or group |
| II-3 | Evidence from multiple time series with or without intervention. Dramatic results in uncontrolled experiments could also be regarded as this type of evidence |
| III | Opinions of respected authorities based on clinical experience; descriptive studies and case reports; or reports of expert committees |

| Grade | Definition |
|---|---|
